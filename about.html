My story

Twelve years of learning that the org only works if the people using it trust it.

I've worked in the Salesforce ecosystem since 2014, hold six Salesforce certifications, and have supported orgs from 200 to 60,000-plus users across enterprise SaaS, ed tech, supply chain, logistics, and nonprofits.

2014 to 2021SalesforceContractor to System Specialist I

I got into Salesforce the way most people my age got into it in 2014: a contract role, no plan, figure it out as you go. I was answering tickets for Salesforce's own internal IT team, learning the platform by fixing what was broken in it. Seven years later I'd worked my way from contractor to Techforce Analyst to System Specialist I, and I was the person leading M&A transitions, moving newly acquired companies onto Salesforce's internal org without breaking anything for the 60,000-plus employees already using it.

That job taught me the thing I still build every engagement around: an org migration succeeds or fails on adoption, not architecture. I built intake flows that cut turnaround time by 20%, and I spent just as much time explaining the new process to the people who'd have to live in it every day.

2021 to 2022InterosAssociate Salesforce Program Analyst

I moved to Interos, a supply-chain risk company, as an Associate Salesforce Program Analyst. The org had years of automation layered on automation: workflow rules next to Process Builder next to half-finished Flows, all doing overlapping things. I spent most of that year consolidating flows and retiring the redundant ones, which sounds unglamorous until you've watched a record trigger four different automations because nobody wanted to be the one who deleted the old ones.

2022 to 2025NewselaSalesforce Administrator

At Newsela, an ed tech company, I was the Salesforce Administrator for 500 users across two orgs. The sales team was booking meetings by hand and re-keying lead data into Salesloft after the fact. I built the integration that closed that loop. Leads flow straight from Salesforce into Salesloft cadences, reps book meetings from the record, and manual entry dropped 40%. It's the kind of project that's invisible when it works: nobody notices the busywork that stopped happening.

2026 to presentTalution GroupConsultant

Now I consult through Talution Group, embedded with a national freight carrier that runs three Salesforce orgs for 5,000-plus users. I manage Sales Cloud, Service Cloud, Experience Cloud, and Slack for the client. Most of the work is paying down technical debt and resolving Flow errors, alongside building relationships and process across business groups that haven't always talked to each other.

Pro bonoDigital Aid SeattleSalesforce Consultant and Project Manager

Alongside that, since 2026 I've been a pro bono Salesforce Consultant and Project Manager for Digital Aid Seattle, architecting Salesforce configurations and API integrations for nonprofits that can't afford a consultant, an IT professional, or a project manager on staff.

Pro bono work isn't a side project for me. Across every nonprofit I've worked with, it's ten nonprofit engagements and 400-plus volunteer hours at this point, same standards as any client that's paying an invoice. One of those, a scholarship and donor CRM automation, ran three years and cut manual data entry by 95% for a team that used to spend entire days re-keying applications by hand.

What I've learned, twelve years in

Most of my work starts with a conversation, not a config screen. The technically correct solution and the one a team will actually use are not always the same thing, and figuring out where they diverge is most of the job. I care about data governance and security because I've cleaned up too many orgs where nobody did, and I care about automation because I've watched good people burn hours on work a Flow could do in seconds.

The best compliment I get after a project isn't "impressive." It's "I didn't notice the change. It just works now."
