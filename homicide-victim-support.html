For every family touched by homicide

Everyone will tell you about the defendant.
This page is about you.

Families of homicide and murder victims have legal rights in every U.S. state. Each state has victims' rights laws, a majority of them in the state constitution, covering notice of hearings, being present and heard at sentencing, input on plea deals, and restitution. Every state also runs a compensation program that can help pay for the funeral and counseling.

After a homicide, a family is usually handed a case number and very little else. Every state has victims' rights laws, and most have written them into the state constitution. Many families are never told what those rights are, or that some of them depend on asking, sometimes by a deadline. If someone you love was murdered or killed by another person, advocates and court paperwork may call you a survivor of homicide or a co-victim. Those words mean you, and the rights on this page are yours.

The checklist nobody hands you

Your state has victims' rights laws

All fifty do, and a majority have gone further and put those rights in the state constitution. Ask the prosecutor's office for the list in writing. Many states require them to.

Your state has a compensation program

Every state runs one, supported in part by federal funds under the Victims of Crime Act of 1984. It can help with funeral costs and counseling for surviving family. Deadlines vary by state, so ask early.

If the case is federal

When the U.S. Department of Justice prosecutes, the Crime Victims' Rights Act, 18 U.S.C. § 3771, applies. Each U.S. Attorney's Office has a victim-witness coordinator. Ask for them by title.

The U.S. Constitution lists the rights of the accused. It still does not mention victims at all.

04 The case, stage by stage

Nobody hands a family a map. Here is the whole road, and the one thing you can do at each turn.

01 Investigation
Detectives build the file. No charges yet, and often no updates for weeks at a time.
Ask for one named point of contact. Ask what you may and may not share publicly. What to expect while no one is charged.
02 Arrest and charging
The prosecutor, not the family, decides the charges. Many states route felony charges through a grand jury, which is closed.
Submit your written request for notice of all proceedings now. This is the request that switches everything else on.
03 Arraignment and bail
The defendant enters a plea and the court sets release conditions. It is short and it is fast.
You may attend. Safety concerns about release should go to the prosecutor in writing beforehand.
04 Pretrial and plea talks
Many homicide cases end here, not at trial. Hearings get continued, sometimes for a year or more.
Ask to be consulted before any offer is made. In some states that consultation is a right you have to request, so request it in writing.
05 Trial
Weeks of testimony, and photographs you may not want to see. Witnesses are often kept out until they testify, though many states exempt the victim's family. Ask whether that rule reaches you.
Ask what exhibits are coming, and when. Bring a support person. You are allowed to leave the room.
06 Sentencing
The judge hears from both sides, then imposes the sentence and any restitution order.
This is your victim impact statement. Write it for the record, not for the defendant. Read the guide, with examples.
07 Appeals
A conviction is not the end. Appeals and post-conviction petitions can run for years.
Ask the appellate unit to keep you on notice. Appeals are often handled by a different office or unit than your trial prosecutor.
08 Parole and release
Hearings can arrive decades later, often by a single letter to an old address.
Register for automated notification, and update your address every time you move. Every time. The parole hearing guide.
05 What you may be owed

Compensation, from the state

Every state runs a crime victim compensation program, funded in part through the federal Victims of Crime Act of 1984. After a homicide these programs commonly help with funeral and burial costs, counseling for surviving family, lost income and crime scene cleanup.

What is covered, the ceiling on an award, and the filing deadline are all set state by state. Apply even if you are unsure you qualify, and ask your victim advocate for your state's deadline in writing.

Restitution, from the person convicted

Restitution is ordered by the court against the person convicted, and it is a separate thing from compensation. Documented losses are what a judge can order repaid, which is why the receipts matter from the first week.

Collecting restitution after sentencing is its own process, and usually its own fight. Ask the prosecutor's victim advocate which statute governs the order in your case and how collection works.

A civil claim, from anyone responsible

A wrongful death lawsuit is a civil case your family brings, separate from the prosecution. It can reach the person who did it and sometimes others who share responsibility, such as a landlord, business or government agency.

It has its own deadline, usually two years from the death, and in most states that clock keeps running during the criminal case. Wrongful death claims and deadlines by state

Insurance, a GoFundMe, compensation and restitution can all exist at once, and one can reduce another. Tell the compensation program what else you have received, because an undisclosed payment is a common reason a claim gets clawed back later. Your state's program sets its own rules, so ask the victim advocate for them in writing.

The defendant gets an attorney on day one. The family gets a pamphlet, if anyone remembers to hand it over. That gap is not an accident, and it is not permanent.

07 Common questions

Questions families ask after a homicide

Do families of murder victims have legal rights?

Yes. Every state has victims' rights laws, and a majority have written them into the state constitution. Some of those rights only switch on once you ask, so ask the prosecutor's office for your list of rights in writing and keep a copy. When the U.S. Department of Justice prosecutes, the federal Crime Victims' Rights Act, 18 U.S.C. § 3771, applies.

Who pays for the funeral when someone is murdered?

Every state runs a crime victim compensation program, funded in part through the federal Victims of Crime Act of 1984. After a homicide these programs commonly help with funeral and burial costs, counseling for surviving family, lost income and crime scene cleanup. Limits and filing deadlines are set state by state, so apply early and keep itemized receipts.

Do families of murder victims get compensation?

Yes, in every state. State crime victim compensation programs, funded in part by the federal Victims of Crime Act, help with funeral costs, counseling and lost income. Separately, a judge can order the person convicted to pay restitution for documented losses. Tell the program about insurance or fundraiser money, because one payment can reduce another.

Can I speak at the sentencing?

Yes. Every state gives victims' families some right to be heard at sentencing, usually through a victim impact statement given in writing, out loud, or both. In some states the right only attaches if you ask, so tell the prosecutor in writing that you want to be heard.

How long does a murder trial take?

The trial itself often runs for weeks of testimony. Getting to trial takes much longer: continuances often push a homicide case a year or more past the arrest, and most cases end in a plea rather than a trial. Appeals and post-conviction petitions can go on for years after sentencing.

Who should I call first after a homicide?

Call the prosecutor's victim advocate yourself rather than waiting to be called. Nearly every prosecutor's and district attorney's office has one. For confidential referrals in any state, call VictimConnect at 1-855-484-2846. In immediate danger, call 911. For 24/7 crisis support, call or text 988.
