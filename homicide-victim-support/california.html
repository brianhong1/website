State guide

Your rights in California

Families of murder victims in California have constitutional rights under Marsy's Law, Article I, Section 28: notice of proceedings and parole hearings, being heard at plea, sentencing and parole, conferring with the prosecutor, refusing defense interviews, and restitution in every case. A victim's spouse, parents, children and siblings all count as victims. Many rights apply on request, so ask in writing.

Marsy's Law, Proposition 9, approved November 4, 2008

California voters wrote 17 victims' rights into the state constitution in 2008, and the parole rules that came with them are some of the strongest in the country. Here is what that means for a homicide victim's family, in plain words.

01

What you are entitled to

Section 28(e) counts a homicide victim's spouse, parents, children, siblings and guardian as victims in their own right, so each of you can assert these. Several only operate on request. Ask the prosecutor in writing, and keep the copy.

  • 01 To be treated with fairness and respect for your privacy and dignity, and to be free from intimidation, harassment and abuse Section 28(b)(1). It runs through the whole case, from the investigation to the last parole hearing.
  • 02 To be reasonably protected from the defendant, and to have your family's safety considered when bail is set Sections 28(b)(2) and (3). Tell the prosecutor before any bail hearing if you have safety concerns, so they can put them in front of the judge.
  • 03 To refuse an interview, deposition or discovery request from the defense Section 28(b)(5). If you do agree to talk, you can set reasonable conditions on how the interview is done. You can also ask that information which could be used to locate or harass your family be kept from the defense (28(b)(4)).
  • 04 On request, to confer with the prosecutor about the charges, and to be told before any pretrial disposition of the case Section 28(b)(6). In practice this is your right to hear about a plea offer before it is final. Ask for it in writing early, because it depends on the request.
  • 05 On request, to notice of all public proceedings and all parole proceedings, and to be present at them Section 28(b)(7).
  • 06 To be heard at any proceeding involving release, plea, sentencing, parole, or any right of yours Section 28(b)(8). At sentencing, Penal Code 1191.1 gives the next of kin the right to appear personally or through an attorney and express their views, and the court must consider them. This is the right behind your victim impact statement.
  • 07 To a speedy trial and a prompt and final conclusion of the case and any related post-judgment proceedings Section 28(b)(9). If a continuance is requested and you object, tell the prosecutor in writing before the hearing.
  • 08 To give the probation officer information about the crime's impact before sentencing, and to receive the pre-sentence report on request Sections 28(b)(10) and (11). Portions made confidential by law are withheld.
  • 09 To restitution, ordered in every case where a victim suffers a loss Section 28(b)(13). The constitution requires the court to order it regardless of the sentence, and money collected from the offender goes to victim restitution first. Keep every receipt from the first week on.
  • 10 To be informed of parole procedures, take part in the parole process, and be notified of release Sections 28(b)(15) and (16). The parole board must consider your family's safety before any release decision.
  • 11 To be informed of all of these rights Section 28(b)(17). Penal Code 679.026 requires law enforcement and the prosecutor to give you a written list, known as the "Marsy Rights" card. If nobody did, ask for it.
02 When a right is ignored

California's constitution goes further than most. Under Section 28(c)(1), you, your retained attorney, a lawful representative, or the prosecutor at your request can enforce these rights in the trial court or the appellate court handling the case "as a matter of right," and the court must act promptly. It does not create a claim for money damages against the state or its employees.

1

Put it in writing, the same day

Email the prosecutor's office or its victim witness assistance center. Name the right, cite the subsection of Section 28(b), give the date it was missed, and say what you want done. A dated email is the record that matters months later.

2

Raise it in court, not the hallway

Ask the prosecutor to raise the violation on the record, or raise it yourself through an attorney. Section 28(c) lets you go to the appellate court as well, but only a ruling on the record gives it something to review.

3

If you want legal help

You do not need a lawyer to use these rights. Section 28(c) lets you enforce them yourself, or ask the prosecutor to. If you want someone on your side, keep in mind the prosecutor represents the People of California, not your family, and a private attorney can appear for you. The state's free, confidential victims' resource line can answer questions and give referrals. It is listed below.

03 Compensation in California

The program

The California Victim Compensation Board, CalVCB, runs the program under Government Code 13950 and following. For a homicide it can help with funeral and burial costs, mental health treatment for surviving family, crime scene cleanup, relocation, and loss of support for the victim's dependents, after insurance and other sources have paid.

File within seven years of the crime, or within seven years of learning the death resulted from a crime, whichever is later (Gov. Code 13953). The board can extend that for good cause, including emotional harm during the trial or at a parole hearing.

CalVCB says it may pay up to $12,818 for a funeral, burial or memorial service, and up to $70,000 in total per victim, after other sources have been applied. Confirm the current limits with CalVCB before you plan around them.

Before you file

Gather the itemized funeral statement, counseling invoices, proof of the victim's income if anyone depended on it, and anything already paid by insurance or a fundraiser. CalVCB pays only what no other source covers, and an undisclosed payment is a common reason a claim is reduced or has to be repaid later. Your county victim witness assistance center can help you fill out the application.

File even if something is missing, and ask what can be supplemented afterward. A filed claim with gaps beats a complete claim filed past the deadline.

04 If the case is unsolved

California has no statute of limitations on murder. Under Penal Code 799(a), a prosecution for any offense punishable by death or life imprisonment can begin at any time, so the case does not expire.

1

Keep one named contact

Get the detective's name, direct number, email and the case number in writing. When that detective retires or moves units, ask who owns the case now and write it down. Send every tip you hear to that person, in writing, so it lands in the file.

2

Keep your rights active

Your Section 28 rights do not lapse while a case is open, and the seven-year CalVCB clock runs from when you learned the death resulted from a crime. Keep the prosecutor's office and CalVCB updated with your address, so notice reaches you if there is an arrest years from now.

3

Before you fund a reward or hire an investigator

Talk to a California attorney and the detective first. A reward offer can be a binding promise, so the wording matters: who qualifies, whether it pays on arrest or conviction, and what happens if no one claims it. A private investigator should be licensed in California and work with the detective, because interviewing witnesses first can hand the defense something to attack.

The cold case guide
Parole, years later

California parole hearings are where many families end up. On request to the Department of Corrections and Rehabilitation, the Board of Parole Hearings must notify you at least 90 days before a hearing (Penal Code 3043). The victim's next of kin, family members and two designated representatives can appear in person or through an attorney. When parole is denied, Penal Code 3041.5 sets the next hearing 15, 10, 7, 5 or 3 years out, and the board must consider your views in choosing. To attend, tell the board at least 15 days before the hearing, according to CDCR's victim services office. How to prepare for a parole hearing

Even a sentence of life without parole may come back before the board. Under rules in effect as of October 2026 (California Code of Regulations, Title 15, Sections 2840 to 2859), the Board of Parole Hearings reviews people who have served at least 25 years, including those serving life without parole, to decide whether to recommend that the Governor commute the sentence or the court recall and resentence it (Penal Code 4801 and 1172.1). A recommendation is not a release; the Governor or the court still decides. Registered victims are notified of these hearings, can attend, and can send a statement up until the panel decides, according to a Prison Law Office summary of the rules. Families and prosecutors are challenging the rules in court (Chavez v. Board of Parole Hearings, Sacramento Superior Court, filed July 15, 2026). Registering with CDCR's Office of Victim and Survivor Rights and Services is how the notice reaches you, so do it even if the sentence was life without parole.

06 Common questions

Questions about California victims' rights

What rights do murder victims' families have in California?

Under Marsy's Law, California Constitution Article I, Section 28, a homicide victim's spouse, parents, children, siblings and guardian are victims in their own right. They can receive notice of proceedings and parole hearings, be present, be heard at release, plea, sentencing and parole, confer with the prosecutor, refuse a defense interview, receive restitution, and expect a speedy and final conclusion of the case.

What is Marsy's Law in California?

Marsy's Law is Proposition 9, approved by California voters on November 4, 2008. It rewrote Article I, Section 28 of the state constitution to list 17 victims' rights, let victims enforce them in court as a matter of right, require restitution in every case with a loss, and lengthen the time between parole hearings for people serving life terms.

Can I speak at the sentencing in California?

Yes. Section 28(b)(8) of the constitution gives you the right to be heard at sentencing, and Penal Code 1191.1 gives a murder victim's next of kin the right to appear in person or through an attorney and express their views. The court must consider what you say. Tell the prosecutor in writing that you want to speak.

How often are parole hearings for murder in California?

When the Board of Parole Hearings denies parole, Penal Code 3041.5 sets the next hearing 15 years later, unless it finds by clear and convincing evidence that a shorter wait of 10, 7, 5 or 3 years is enough. The board must consider the victim's views when it sets the date. Families get at least 90 days' notice on request.

Does California pay for a murder victim's funeral?

The California Victim Compensation Board can help pay for a funeral, burial or memorial service after other sources have paid, up to $12,818 according to CalVCB. Claims are generally due within seven years under Government Code 13953. Call CalVCB at 800-777-9229 to confirm the current limits.

What do I do if my rights are ignored in California?

Put it in writing the same day to the prosecutor's office, naming the right and the date. Section 28(c) lets you, your attorney, or the prosecutor at your request enforce your rights in the trial or appellate court handling the case as a matter of right, and the court must act promptly.

Is there a statute of limitations on murder in California?

No. Penal Code 799 allows a prosecution for any offense punishable by death or life imprisonment, which includes murder, to begin at any time. An unsolved homicide in California does not expire.
