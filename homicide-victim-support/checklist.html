Practical guide

The checklist nobody hands you

After a murder, a family's first three tasks are to write down the case number and the detective's direct line, ask the prosecutor's office for its victim advocate, and keep every receipt. Within weeks, apply for crime victim compensation and request notice of court dates in writing. The list below runs from the first days through parole.

Families come out of a homicide with no idea what happens next, and the system is not built to explain it. This is the list of things that need attention, grouped by when they tend to come up. It is for anyone whose family member was murdered or killed, the people victim services call survivors of homicide or co-victims.

Read the part you are in and ignore the rest. Nothing here has to be done today, in order, or by you alone. Hand a section to someone who offered to help and wanted a job.

06 Things nobody warns you about

The paperwork outlasts the sympathy

Casseroles stop after a month. The forms, hearings and phone calls go on for years. Plan your support for month eight, not week one.

You will share a hallway with their family

Courthouses are small. Ask your advocate about a separate waiting area. Some courthouses have one, and almost nobody offers it unprompted.

Your person becomes a case file

Photographs, records and testimony enter the public record. It is jarring, and it is not a reflection of who they were. You are allowed to correct people.

07 Common questions

Questions families ask after a murder

What should I do first after a family member is murdered?

Three things: write down the case number and the detective's direct line, ask the prosecutor's office for the victim advocate by name, and keep every receipt, starting now. Everything else can wait until those are done. The victim advocate is the most useful call available to you, and most families are never told one exists.

Do I have to clean up the crime scene myself?

No. If it happened in your home, cleanup becomes your responsibility once police release the scene, but you do not have to do it yourself. Specialist companies do this work, and many state crime victim compensation programs help pay for it. Ask the victim advocate about coverage before anyone touches anything.

Can I get a copy of the autopsy report?

Often, yes, but the rules vary by state. In many states the family can request the autopsy report from the medical examiner or coroner, though it may be held back while the investigation is open. It does not have to be read by everyone, by anyone, or right away. Decide as a family who, if anyone, reads it.

Do I have to talk to reporters after a murder?

No. You can say no, say nothing, or name one person to speak for the family. Anything said publicly can surface later in court. The police department's public information officer can field media calls for you, so ask them to.

How will I know if the person who killed my family member is released?

Register for automated custody notification yourself. Most states have a system that alerts you to transfers, release and escape. Then keep your address current with the prosecutor's office, the state corrections department and the parole board, because notices go to the last address on file and a parole hearing can come decades later.
