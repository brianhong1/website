Guide

When it has been years

A homicide case goes cold when investigators run out of leads, but it is not closed, and there is generally no time limit on charging murder. Families can ask in writing for a case review, ask whether evidence can be retested with newer DNA methods, keep their own record of the case, and keep it in public view. If a federal agency investigated, a 2022 law gives families the right to apply for a formal review.

Unsolved, and still open

Detectives retire. Prosecutors move on. Evidence sits in a property room. Often the only people who have been there for the whole case are the family. That is a burden, and it is also why families are so often the reason an old case gets a second look.

01

What "cold" means

It does not mean closed, and it does not mean forgotten.

  • 01 The leads ran out A case usually goes cold when the original detectives have followed every lead they had and none of it has led to a charge.
  • 02 It stays open An unsolved homicide stays open. There is generally no statute of limitations for murder, so a charge can come whenever the evidence is there.
  • 03 Someone should own it Many larger departments have a cold case unit. In smaller ones, an old case may sit with whichever detective inherited it. Find out who that is.
  • 04 Science keeps moving DNA methods that did not exist when the crime happened can now get results from small or degraded samples. Evidence collected years ago can still matter.
02 Ask for a review

Write it down. A letter creates a record that a phone call does not, and it outlasts staff turnover.

1

Find who has the case

Ask the agency that investigated who is assigned to the case now, and whether it is with a cold case unit. Ask the prosecutor's office whether it has a cold case unit or a victim advocate for older cases.

2

Ask for a review in writing

Ask that the case be reviewed, if possible by someone who did not work it the first time, and ask when it was last reviewed. Repeat the request every year. The anniversary is an easy date to remember.

3

Ask about the evidence

Ask whether the physical evidence is still preserved, and whether it has been tested with current DNA methods. Ask whether forensic genetic genealogy has been considered. Federal policy allows it for homicides.

Some prosecutors have federal money for exactly this. The Bureau of Justice Assistance funds cold case prosecutions where a suspect DNA profile already exists. It is fair to ask whether your county has applied.

03 If a federal agency investigated

Who can apply

  • The murder happened on or after January 1, 1970, and more than three years before you apply.
  • A federal law enforcement agency investigated it, such as the FBI or a military criminal investigative service.
  • All probative leads have been exhausted, and no likely perpetrator has been identified.
  • You are an immediate family member: a parent, parent-in-law, grandparent, grandparent-in-law, sibling, spouse, child or stepchild, or someone similarly situated.

Most homicides are investigated by local police and are not covered. Some states have considered similar laws for local cases, so ask your advocate whether yours has one.

What the law requires

The Homicide Victims' Families' Rights Act of 2021, signed into law in August 2022, lets a family member apply in writing to the agency that investigated. The agency must confirm it received the application, then finish a review of the case file within six months. It can extend that once, by up to six more months.

The review must be done by someone who did not work the original investigation. It looks for missed investigative steps, witnesses to interview again, and evidence that has not had every appropriate forensic test. If it finds that a full reinvestigation would likely turn up new leads, one follows. The agency must keep the family informed and meet with them to explain its decision.

If the review finds no reinvestigation is warranted, the agency generally does not have to review the case again for five years unless newly discovered, materially significant evidence comes in.

04 Keeping the case in view

Attention brings tips, and tips solve cold cases.

1

Anniversaries

An anniversary story, a vigil or a billboard gives a reporter a reason to run the case again. Bring a photograph and the number for the tip line.

2

Rewards

Ask whether a reward exists, whether it can be raised, and whether a local Crime Stoppers program will publicize it and take tips anonymously. In Oregon, the statewide program closed in 2026, so see the Oregon guide.

3

Online

A page or post with a photograph, the date and place, and the number to call is safe and useful. Keep crime details out of it, and point tips to the detective rather than to yourself.

If someone is still missing

If your loved one disappeared and was never found, make sure they are entered in NamUs, the national missing and unidentified persons system, and ask whether family DNA reference samples have been collected. That is how unidentified remains are matched to families.

05 Keep the file yourself

What to keep

  • Every case number, and every name with a date: detectives, prosecutors, advocates.
  • A copy of every letter you send and every reply.
  • News coverage, with dates.
  • What you know about where the evidence is held, and when it was last tested.

Keep a copy with a second family member. The file should outlast any one person.

Why it matters

Police departments and prosecutors' offices turn over. A family file showing who said what, and when, is how a new detective learns the history of the case quickly, and how you notice when a promised review never happened.

You do not have to carry this alone. Parents of Murdered Children has members whose cases have been unsolved for decades.

06 Common questions

Questions about cold cases

Is there a statute of limitations on murder?

Generally, no. Murder can be charged however long ago it happened, and federal law allows a capital offense to be charged at any time. Lesser homicide charges, such as manslaughter, can have time limits in some states.

How do I get a cold case reopened?

Write to the agency that investigated and ask for a case review, ideally by someone who did not work it before. Ask whether the evidence has been tested with current DNA methods. Repeat the request every year and keep copies. If a federal agency investigated, the Homicide Victims' Families' Rights Act gives immediate family members the right to apply for a formal review.

What is the Homicide Victims' Families' Rights Act?

A 2022 federal law that lets immediate family members apply for a review of an unsolved murder investigated by a federal agency, if it happened on or after January 1, 1970, and more than three years have passed. The agency must finish the review within six months, extendable once, and reinvestigate if the review finds new leads are likely. It does not cover cases investigated only by local police.

Can DNA solve an old murder?

Often it can. Newer methods can test smaller and older samples, and forensic genetic genealogy has identified suspects in decades-old cases. Federal policy allows genetic genealogy for homicides and for identifying unknown remains. Ask whether the evidence in your case is preserved and when it was last tested.

Does a cold case mean the police stopped investigating?

Not exactly. It means the active leads ran out. The case stays open, and a new tip, a witness who changes their mind, or a new forensic test can restart it. Ask who is assigned to the case now and when it will next be reviewed.

Can families do their own investigating?

Keep records and pass on everything you hear, but leave the investigating to detectives. Confronting someone can put you in danger, and information gathered informally can be harder to use in court. Send tips to the detective in writing so there is a record.
