State guide

Your rights in Florida

Families of murder victims in Florida have constitutional rights under Marsy's Law, Article I, Section 16(b): notice of and presence at all public proceedings, even as a witness, being heard at release, plea, sentencing and parole, conferring with the prosecutor on any plea, full restitution, and a prompt end to the case. The next of kin of a homicide victim count as victims. Many rights apply on request.

Marsy's Law, adopted November 2018

Florida voters rewrote the victims' rights section of the state constitution in 2018. It is self-executing, starts at the moment of the crime, and sets hard timelines for appeals. Here is what that means for a homicide victim's family, in plain words.

01

What you are entitled to

Section 16(e) counts "the next of kin of a homicide victim" as a victim, and says the rights begin at the time of the crime. Section 16(d) makes them self-executing, so they need no further law to apply. The rights in 16(b)(6) operate on request. Ask the state attorney's office in writing and keep the copy.

  • 01 To due process, and to be treated with fairness and respect for your dignity, free from intimidation, harassment and abuse Section 16(b)(1) and (2).
  • 02 To be reasonably protected from the accused, and to have your family's safety considered when bail and release conditions are set Section 16(b)(3) and (4). At a first appearance, a reasonable attempt by the agency to notify you and convey your views to the court satisfies these rights (16(b)(7)), so tell the detective or advocate your concerns early.
  • 03 To prevent disclosure of information or records that could be used to locate or harass you or your family Section 16(b)(5). This protects information like your address. It does not keep a victim's name secret. See the note on names below.
  • 04 On request, to notice of and to be present at all public proceedings, including trial, plea and sentencing, even if you will be a witness Section 16(b)(6)a. This overrides the usual rule that keeps witnesses out of the courtroom, which matters in homicide cases where a family member often testifies.
  • 05 On request, to be heard at any public proceeding involving release, plea, sentencing or parole Section 16(b)(6)b. At sentencing, s. 921.143 lets the next of kin appear and make a statement under oath, or submit a written statement under oath through the state attorney. This is your victim impact statement.
  • 06 On request, to confer with the prosecutor about any plea agreement, release, restitution, sentencing or other disposition Section 16(b)(6)c. Confer means you are consulted, not that you decide. Ask for the conversation before any plea hearing is set.
  • 07 On request, to give impact information for the presentence investigation, and to receive a copy of the presentence report Section 16(b)(6)d and e, except portions made confidential by law.
  • 08 On request, to be informed of postconviction and clemency processes, take part in them, and be notified before any release Section 16(b)(6)f, g and h. The release authority must extend the right to be heard to anyone harmed by the offender.
  • 09 To full and timely restitution in every case and from each convicted offender, for all losses suffered directly and indirectly Section 16(b)(9). Keep every receipt from the first week on.
  • 10 To proceedings free from unreasonable delay, and a prompt and final conclusion of the case Section 16(b)(10). The state attorney can file a good-faith demand for a speedy trial, and the court must hold a calendar call within 15 days and set trial 5 to 60 days later unless it makes specific findings. A separate statute, s. 960.0015, also allows a demand once the court has granted at least three defense continuances over the state's objection. If delays are piling up, ask the prosecutor whether either applies. State appeals and collateral attacks must finish within two years in non-capital cases and five years in capital cases, unless a court explains why not.
  • 11 To be informed of these rights, and that you can seek the advice of an attorney about them Section 16(b)(11). The information must be given to you on a card or by other effective means. If nobody did, ask for it.
02 When a right is ignored

Under Section 16(c), you, your retained attorney, a lawful representative, or the state attorney at your request may assert and seek enforcement of these rights "as a matter of right" in any trial or appellate court, or before any other authority with jurisdiction over the case, such as a parole or clemency body. The court must act promptly, give a remedy for the violation, and state its reasons on the record.

1

Put it in writing, the same day

Email the state attorney's victim services office. Name the right and the subsection of Section 16(b), the date it was missed, and what you want done. A dated email is the record that matters months later.

2

Ask for a ruling on the record

Section 16(c) requires the court to state its reasons on the record for any decision about a victim's right. Ask the prosecutor to raise the violation, or raise it through your own attorney, so there is a reasoned ruling that a higher court can review.

3

If you want legal help

You do not need a lawyer to use these rights. Section 16(c) lets you assert them yourself, or ask the state attorney to. If you want someone on your side, keep in mind the state attorney represents the State of Florida, not your family. Section 16(b)(11) says you must be told you can seek an attorney's advice, and a retained attorney can assert your rights for you. The Attorney General's victim services division can help with referrals. It is listed below.

A note on names

Many families assume Marsy's Law keeps a victim's name out of public records. In City of Tallahassee v. Florida Police Benevolent Association, decided November 30, 2023, the Florida Supreme Court held that it does not. Section 16(b)(5) protects information that could be used to locate or harass you, such as an address, but it gives no victim a categorical right to withhold their name. If you need to keep contact details private, ask the advocate which forms to use.

03 Compensation in Florida

The program

The Crime Victim Compensation Program is run by the Attorney General's Bureau of Victim Compensation under Florida Statutes Chapter 960. For a homicide it can help with funeral costs, counseling, and loss of support for the victim's dependents. It is a payer of last resort, after insurance and every other source (s. 960.13(3)).

For crimes on or after October 1, 2019, file within three years of the crime, the death, or the date the death was found to result from the crime, whichever is later (s. 960.07). The crime must have been reported within five days, and awards are capped at $25,000 in total unless the department finds a catastrophic injury (s. 960.13).

Florida requires a showing of serious financial hardship for awards covering lost earnings or support and out-of-pocket losses (s. 960.13(8)). Confirm the current limits and what counts with the Bureau before you file.

Before you file

Gather the itemized funeral statement, counseling invoices, proof of the victim's income if anyone depended on it, and anything already paid by insurance or a fundraiser. The program pays only after every other source, and an undisclosed payment is a common reason a claim is reduced or has to be repaid later. Your state attorney's victim advocate can help with the application.

File even if something is missing, and ask what can be supplemented afterward. A filed claim with gaps beats a complete claim filed past the deadline.

The first two weeks, including what to keep

A separate deadline: wrongful death. A civil lawsuit runs on its own clock, apart from compensation and the criminal case. In Florida that is generally two years from the death. A claim against the person who committed a murder or manslaughter has no deadline, but claims against anyone else keep the two years. Wrongful death deadlines by state

04 If the case is unsolved

Florida has no statute of limitations on murder. Under Florida Statutes 775.15(1), a prosecution for a capital felony, a life felony, or any felony that resulted in a death may be started at any time, so the case does not expire.

1

Keep one named contact

Get the detective's name, direct number, email and the case number in writing. When that detective retires or moves units, ask who owns the case now and write it down. Send every tip you hear to that person, in writing, so it lands in the file.

2

Your rights start now, not at arrest

Section 16(b) rights begin at the time of the crime, not when someone is charged. That includes protection of your contact information and your right to fair treatment during the investigation. If an advocate has not contacted you, ask the investigating agency for its victim advocate.

3

Before you fund a reward or hire an investigator

Talk to a Florida attorney and the detective first. A reward offer can be a binding promise, so the wording matters: who qualifies, whether it pays on arrest or conviction, and what happens if no one claims it. A private investigator should be licensed in Florida and work with the detective, because interviewing witnesses first can hand the defense something to attack.

Parole in Florida

Florida abolished parole for most crimes committed on or after October 1, 1983. The Florida Commission on Offender Review still holds parole hearings for crimes committed before that date and certain later offenses, and it handles conditional release and clemency, according to the state's OPPAGA program summary. If the offender is parole-eligible, you have the right to attend and speak. For most newer cases, what matters is release notification from the Department of Corrections. 06 Common questions

Questions about Florida victims' rights

What rights do murder victims' families have in Florida?

Under Marsy's Law, Florida Constitution Article I, Section 16(b), the next of kin of a homicide victim can receive notice of and attend all public proceedings, even as a witness, be heard at release, plea, sentencing and parole, confer with the prosecutor about any plea, receive the presentence report, get full restitution, and expect a prompt and final conclusion of the case.

What is Marsy's Law in Florida?

Marsy's Law is the 2018 amendment that rewrote Article I, Section 16(b) through (e) of the Florida Constitution. It lists victims' rights that begin at the time of the crime, makes them self-executing, lets victims enforce them in any trial or appellate court as a matter of right, and sets deadlines for appeals.

Can I speak at the sentencing in Florida?

Yes. Section 16(b)(6)b of the constitution gives you the right to be heard at sentencing on request, and Florida Statutes 921.143 lets the next of kin of a homicide victim appear and make a statement under oath, or submit a written statement under oath through the state attorney's office.

Does Marsy's Law keep a murder victim's name private in Florida?

No. In City of Tallahassee v. Florida Police Benevolent Association, decided November 30, 2023, the Florida Supreme Court held that Marsy's Law gives no victim a categorical right to withhold their name. It protects information that could be used to locate or harass a victim or their family, such as an address.

Does Florida pay for a murder victim's funeral?

The Attorney General's Bureau of Victim Compensation can help with funeral costs, counseling and loss of support under Florida Statutes Chapter 960, as a payer of last resort. Claims for crimes since October 1, 2019 are due within three years, and total awards are capped at $25,000 in most cases. Call 1-800-226-6667 to confirm current limits.

What do I do if my rights are ignored in Florida?

Put it in writing the same day to the state attorney's office, naming the right and the date. Section 16(c) lets you, your attorney, or the state attorney at your request enforce your rights in any trial or appellate court as a matter of right, and the court must act promptly and state its reasons on the record.

Is there a statute of limitations on murder in Florida?

No. Florida Statutes 775.15(1) allows a prosecution for a capital felony, a life felony, or any felony that resulted in a death to begin at any time. An unsolved homicide in Florida does not expire.
