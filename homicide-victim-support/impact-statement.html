Guide

Writing your victim impact statement

A victim impact statement is your chance to tell the judge at sentencing who you lost and what the murder has done to your family. Every state allows one. You can submit it in writing, read it aloud, or have someone read it for you. Speak about the loss, and leave out threats and new facts about the crime.

Given at sentencing, in every state

For most of a case, other people do the talking. The impact statement is the part that is yours: the judge hears, in your words, who you lost and what the loss has done. There is no wrong way to feel about writing it, and no rule that it has to be polished.

Where sentencing falls in the case

01

What it is, and what it is for

It is generally not testimony, and you are generally not cross-examined on it. Georgia is an exception: there, impact evidence is given as testimony and can be cross-examined, so read the Georgia guide first if your case is there. It is your account, given to the court.

  • 01 It tells the judge what the crime cost The emotional, physical and financial harm to you, your family and the people close to the person you lost.
  • 02 The judge takes it into account It is weighed alongside everything else the law requires at sentencing. It will not decide the sentence on its own, and it is not meant to.
  • 03 It supports restitution Financial losses you describe, backed by receipts, help the court set the amount the defendant is ordered to repay.
  • 04 It is a right, not a favor Every state gives victims' families some right to be heard at sentencing. In some states it only attaches if you ask, so tell the prosecutor in writing that you want to be heard.
02 Ways to give it

You can choose the form that you can actually get through. Many families do more than one: a written statement for the record, and a shorter version read aloud.

1

Write it and submit it

A letter to the judge, a personal narrative, or the court's own form. It goes to the prosecutor or the probation office ahead of sentencing, so ask who and by when.

2

Read it aloud in court

You speak at the sentencing hearing, usually from a podium facing the judge. You can read what you wrote word for word. Nobody expects you to memorize it.

3

Have someone read it for you

A victim advocate, the prosecutor, or another family member can read your words. Arrange it before the hearing, not at the courtroom door.

If you want to show a photograph, play a recording, or appear by video, ask well in advance. Courts differ on what they allow.

03

What to put in it

You do not need all of these. Pick the ones that are true for you, and say them plainly. Specific details carry more weight than big words.

  • 01 Who they were Not a eulogy, just enough that the judge sees a person rather than a case number. One habit, one plan they had, one thing only they did.
  • 02 How you found out Many families describe the moment they were told. Include it only if you want to.
  • 03 What changed in ordinary days Holidays, the empty chair, the drive you can no longer make, the phone call that does not come.
  • 04 Your health Sleep, appetite, panic, counseling, medication, a condition that got worse. These are real effects, and the court can hear them.
  • 05 Children and the rest of the family What the children ask, who is raising them now, the relationships that changed or broke under the weight.
  • 06 Money and work Funeral costs, lost income, leave you took, a job you lost, a move you had to make. Keep the receipts, because this is the part that feeds restitution.
  • 07 What you want the court to understand A closing thought in your own words. It can be short.
Example lines

These are illustrations of tone and detail, not a script. Replace them with what is true for you.

  • "[Name] was the person everyone called when a car broke down at two in the morning, and [he/she/they] always came."
  • "I found out on a Tuesday morning, from a detective standing on my porch."
  • "I have not slept through a night since March."
  • "Our daughter is seven. She still sets a place for her father at dinner."
  • "I took eleven weeks of unpaid leave, and I have not gone back to the job I had."
04 What to leave out

Anger belongs in it if anger is true. What can hurt you is anything that gives the defense a reason to object, or the judge a reason to set your words aside.

1

Threats and slurs

Wishing harm on the defendant, or profanity aimed at them, can get a statement cut short. Say what was taken from you instead.

2

New facts about the crime

If you know something about what happened that never came out in the case, tell the prosecutor before sentencing. A court generally cannot rely on new facts the defense had no chance to answer.

3

A sentence, in a death-penalty case

In a capital case, family members may not tell the jury what sentence they want, or give their opinion of the crime or the defendant. In other cases, many states let you say what sentence you think is fair.

Ask first

Judges differ on whether you may face the defendant and speak to them directly, how long you may speak, and what you may show. Ask the prosecutor what this judge allows, and ask them to read your draft before the hearing.

05 Getting it ready

Ask the advocate

  • When the written statement is due, and to whom.
  • Whether there is a form, a page limit or a time limit.
  • Who else will see it. Written statements usually go to the defense, with identifying details often removed.
  • How many family members may speak, and in what order.

Sentencing dates move. Ask to be told of every change, in writing.

Write it in pieces

Start long before sentencing. Keep a note on your phone and add a line whenever something occurs to you. A statement assembled from months of notes is easier to write, and truer, than one written the night before.

Read it aloud at home, more than once. Bring a support person to the hearing, and a printed copy in large type. If you need to stop partway, you can stop. The judge has heard many families before you.

06 After sentencing

Keep your own copy, and keep your notes. If the sentence allows for parole, the parole board will usually take a statement from the family too, sometimes many years later, and your first statement is the best starting point for it. Register for custody and release notification so that you hear about a hearing in time.

The rules on who may speak, and when, are set by each state. In Texas, for example, the family's spoken statement comes after the sentence is pronounced, so the written statement is the one the judge weighs. The state guides and which rights apply to your case cover the details.

07 Common questions

Questions about victim impact statements

What is a victim impact statement?

A victim impact statement is the family's account, given to the court at sentencing, of who was lost and what the loss has done, including the emotional, physical and financial harm. It is generally not testimony, and you are generally not cross-examined on it. Georgia is an exception: there, impact evidence is given as testimony and can be cross-examined, so read the Georgia guide first if your case is there. Many states require the judge to consider it alongside everything else the law requires, and the financial losses you describe help support restitution.

Do I have to read my victim impact statement out loud in court?

No. You can submit it in writing, read it aloud at the sentencing hearing, or both. A victim advocate, the prosecutor or another family member can read it for you. Arrange that before the hearing, and ask the prosecutor's victim advocate when a written statement is due and to whom.

What should I leave out of a victim impact statement?

Anger belongs in it if anger is true. Leave out threats, slurs and profanity aimed at the defendant, which can get a statement cut short. Leave out new facts about the crime and tell the prosecutor about them before sentencing instead, because a court generally cannot rely on facts the defense had no chance to answer.

Can I give a victim impact statement in a death-penalty case?

Yes, with limits. In a capital case, family members may not tell the jury what sentence they want, or give their opinion of the crime or the defendant, under Bosse v. Oklahoma (2016). In other cases, many states let you say what sentence you think is fair. Ask the prosecutor what this judge allows.

Is a victim impact statement used again at parole?

Often, yes. If the sentence allows for parole, the parole board will usually take a statement from the family too, sometimes many years later, and your first statement is the best starting point for it. Keep your own copy and your notes, and register for custody and release notification so you hear about a hearing in time.

How long should a victim impact statement be?

There is no single rule. Some courts set a page or time limit, some use a form, and judges differ on how long you may speak and what you may show. Ask the prosecutor what this judge allows, and ask them to read your draft before the hearing.
