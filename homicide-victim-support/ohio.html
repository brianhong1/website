State guide

Your rights in Ohio

Families of murder victims in Ohio have constitutional rights under Marsy's Law, Article I, Section 10a, and Revised Code Chapter 2930: notice of public proceedings, being present and heard at plea and sentencing, conferring with the prosecutor, refusing defense interviews, and full restitution. Some apply only on request, so ask the prosecutor in writing.

Marsy's Law, effective February 2018

Ohio put victims' rights into its constitution in 2018, and rewrote the statute behind them in 2023. Here is what that means for a homicide victim's family, in plain words.

01

What you are entitled to

These are rights, not courtesies. Some of them only operate once you ask. On Ohio's victim's rights request form, a right you do not request is treated as waived, though you can request it later (R.C. 2930.04). Ask in writing and keep the copy.

  • 01 To be treated with fairness and respect for your safety, dignity and privacy Section 10a(A)(1).
  • 02 On request, to reasonable and timely notice of all public proceedings, and to be present at them Section 10a(A)(2). The grand jury is the exception. It is closed, and that is not a slight against you.
  • 03 To be heard at any public proceeding involving release, plea, sentencing, disposition or parole, or where one of your rights is at issue Section 10a(A)(3). This is the right behind your victim impact statement, and behind being able to speak at a bail hearing.
  • 04 To reasonable protection from the accused or anyone acting for them Section 10a(A)(4). If anyone contacts or threatens your family, tell the detective and the prosecutor in writing, the same day.
  • 05 On request, to reasonable notice of any release or escape of the accused Section 10a(A)(5). Register with the Ohio Department of Rehabilitation and Correction's Office of Victim Services, listed below, once there is a prison sentence.
  • 06 To refuse a defense interview, deposition or other discovery request Section 10a(A)(6). A subpoena or a court-ordered deposition still applies (R.C. 2930.072). In court, you generally do not have to give your address, phone number or workplace (R.C. 2930.07).
  • 07 To full and timely restitution from the person convicted Section 10a(A)(7). Documented losses are what the court can order repaid, so keep every receipt from the first week on.
  • 08 To proceedings free from unreasonable delay and a prompt conclusion of the case Section 10a(A)(8). If you object to a continuance, the court may grant it only if the delay is reasonable or in the interest of justice, and it must state its reason (R.C. 2930.08). Tell the prosecutor in writing.
  • 09 On request, to confer with the prosecutor Section 10a(A)(9). This is your input on a plea deal. Ask for the conversation in writing before any plea hearing is set. You are consulted, not given a veto.
  • 10 To have a support person with you during proceedings R.C. 2930.09. Ask the victim advocate to arrange it ahead of the hearing rather than at the courtroom door.
  • 11 To be informed, in writing, of all of these rights Section 10a(A)(10). Law enforcement and the prosecutor each carry written notice duties. If nobody gave you anything in writing, ask for it and keep the request.
02 When a right is ignored

Ohio lets you, your lawful representative, or the prosecutor at your request assert a right in the court handling the case and ask for a remedy, and petition the court of appeals if relief is denied. R.C. 2930.011 preserves your ability to assert the constitutional rights in Article I, Section 10a.

1

Put it in writing, the same day

Email the prosecutor's office. Name the right, the date it was missed, and what you want done about it. A dated email is the record that matters months later.

2

Ask for it on the record

A right raised in a hallway is a conversation. A right raised in open court is a ruling, and a ruling is something an appellate court can look at.

3

If you want legal help

You do not need a lawyer to use these rights. You can assert them yourself, or ask the prosecutor to. If you want someone on your side, keep in mind the prosecutor represents the state, not you. Ohio has a nonprofit that represents families at no cost specifically to enforce these rights. It is listed below.

The appeal clocks are short

When you assert a right, the trial court must hear it within ten days and state its reasons for any denial (R.C. 2930.19). If the court denies relief while the case is still pending, an appeal or petition for a writ must be filed within 14 days of when the prosecutor notifies you of the decision, or it will be dismissed. After sentencing, a victim's appeal must be filed within 30 days of the final order. In State v. Barnes, 2026-Ohio-2750, decided July 22, 2026, the Supreme Court of Ohio held that a victim who misses that deadline cannot file a delayed appeal, even though a defendant can. If a right is denied, call the Ohio Crime Victim Justice Center that day.

03 Compensation in Ohio

The program

The Crime Victims Compensation Program is run by the Ohio Attorney General's Crime Victim Services Section under R.C. 2743.51 to 2743.72. It can reimburse funeral and burial costs, counseling, lost wages and other economic losses that insurance does not cover.

File within three years of the crime (R.C. 2743.56). Awards for one victim are capped at $50,000 in total (R.C. 2743.60), and funeral expenses at $7,500 per funeral, which can include up to $500 per family member for lost wages or travel to attend (R.C. 2743.51(N)). Cooperation requirements apply. Confirm the current limits with the Attorney General's office before you file, and ask what receipts they need.

Before you file

Gather the itemized funeral statement, counseling invoices, proof of lost income, and anything already paid by insurance or a fundraiser. Programs reimburse from itemized receipts, and an undisclosed payment is a common reason a claim is reduced or clawed back later.

File even if something is missing, and ask what can be supplemented afterward. A filed claim with gaps beats a complete claim filed past the deadline.

The first two weeks, including what to keep

A separate deadline: wrongful death. A civil lawsuit runs on its own clock, apart from compensation and the criminal case. In Ohio that is two years from the death, and the criminal case does not extend it. Wrongful death deadlines by state

04 If the case is unsolved

Ohio has no statute of limitations on murder. Under R.C. 2901.13(A)(2), there is no time limit to prosecute aggravated murder or murder, so the case does not expire.

1

Keep one named contact

Get the detective's name, direct number, email and the case number in writing. When that detective retires or moves units, ask who owns the case now and write it down. Send every tip you hear to that person, in writing, so it lands in the file.

2

Do not wait on compensation

The three-year compensation clock runs from the crime, not from an arrest. Apply now, even with no suspect, and keep the Attorney General's office updated with your address.

The cold case guide
3

Before you fund a reward or hire an investigator

Talk to an Ohio attorney and the detective first. A reward offer can be a binding promise, so the wording matters: who qualifies, whether it pays on arrest or conviction, and what happens if no one claims it. A private investigator should be licensed in Ohio and work with the detective, because interviewing witnesses first can hand the defense something to attack.

Parole, years later

Many Ohio murder sentences are life with parole eligibility, so the Ohio Parole Board may review the case years from now. In a murder or aggravated murder case, a victim or the victim's representative can require the Board to hold a full board hearing instead of a routine review, by asking through the Office of Victim Services. Other family members can ask too, and the Board votes on it. The Board must give the victim at least 30 days' notice of a full board hearing (R.C. 5149.101). Register with the Office of Victim Services so notices reach you. How to prepare for a parole hearing

06 Common questions

Questions about Ohio victims' rights

What rights do murder victims' families have in Ohio?

Under Marsy's Law, Ohio Constitution Article I, Section 10a, and R.C. Chapter 2930, a homicide victim's family has the right to notice of public proceedings and to be present at them, to be heard at release, plea, sentencing and parole, to be reasonably protected from the accused, to notice of release or escape, to confer with the prosecutor, to refuse a defense interview, to have a support person, to full and timely restitution, to proceedings free from unreasonable delay, and to be informed of these rights.

What is Marsy's Law in Ohio?

Marsy's Law is the victims' rights section of the Ohio Constitution, Article I, Section 10a, in effect since February 2018. The statute behind it, R.C. Chapter 2930, was substantially revised by H.B. 343, effective April 6, 2023. Together they set out the rights a homicide victim's family can assert in an Ohio criminal case.

What do I do if my rights are ignored in Ohio?

Put it in writing the same day: email the prosecutor's office, name the right, the date it was missed, and what you want done. Ohio lets you, your lawful representative, or the prosecutor at your request assert the right in the court handling the case and ask for a remedy. Raising it on the record turns a conversation into a ruling. If the court denies relief, the deadlines are short: 14 days for an appeal while the case is pending and 30 days after sentencing, with no delayed appeal allowed (R.C. 2930.19; State v. Barnes, 2026-Ohio-2750).

Does Ohio pay for a murder victim's funeral?

The Crime Victims Compensation Program, run by the Ohio Attorney General's Crime Victim Services Section under R.C. 2743.51 to 2743.72, can reimburse funeral and burial costs, counseling, lost wages and other economic losses that insurance does not cover. Apply within three years of the crime. Awards are capped at $50,000 in total and $7,500 per funeral (R.C. 2743.56, 2743.60 and 2743.51). Confirm the current limits with the Attorney General's office at 1-800-582-2877.

Who can help me enforce my rights in Ohio?

The prosecutor represents the state, not your family. The Ohio Crime Victim Justice Center gives free legal help when a Marsy's Law right is being violated, at 614-848-8500. Your county prosecutor's victim advocate is the first call for everything else, and Ohio Department of Rehabilitation and Correction victim services, at 1-888-842-8464, handles custody and release notification.

Can I speak at the sentencing in Ohio?

Yes. Article I, Section 10a(A)(3) of the Ohio Constitution gives you the right to be heard at any public proceeding involving release, plea, sentencing, disposition or parole. Most families do this with a victim impact statement, given in writing, aloud, or both. Tell the prosecutor in writing that you want to be heard.
