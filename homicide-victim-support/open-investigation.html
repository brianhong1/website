Guide

When no one has been arrested

During an open homicide investigation, detectives often share very little, and the silence is usually about protecting the case, not ignoring you. You can ask for one named contact and a regular check-in schedule, ask what you may say in public, and keep a written log of every call. Most of the police file stays closed while the case is open.

Before an arrest, in every state

The days after a murder are full of people, and then the phones stop. When no one has been charged, the quiet can feel like nobody is working the case. Sometimes that is true. More often the work is happening where you cannot see it.

01

Why detectives go quiet

The silence is hard to bear, and most of the time it is on purpose.

  • 01 They are protecting details only the killer knows Investigators often hold back facts about the crime. If one of those facts turns up in a tip or an interview, it tells them they are talking to the right person. Once it is public, it is useless to them.
  • 02 Anyone close to the victim can be a witness Detectives interview family and friends early, and sometimes more than once. It is routine. It does not mean you are a suspect, and it is not a reason to stop cooperating.
  • 03 They may not know yet Lab results, phone records and warrants take weeks or months. A detective with nothing new to tell you may simply tell you nothing.
  • 04 Caseloads are heavy Many homicide detectives carry several open cases at once. A family that checks in politely, on a schedule, tends to stay at the front of their mind.
02 What you can ask for

You are not asking a favor. These are reasonable requests, and putting them in writing starts a paper trail.

1

One named contact

Ask who your point of contact is, and get a direct line and an email address. If that detective is reassigned or retires, ask who takes over the case.

2

A check-in schedule

Ask for an update on a set schedule, monthly for example, even when there is nothing new. "No change" is still an answer, and it tells you the case is being looked at.

3

What you may say in public

Ask what you may share with reporters, on social media and with friends, and what you should keep to yourself for now.

If calls and emails go unanswered for weeks, write to the detective's supervisor, politely, with the dates you tried. A victim advocate can often help get a response.

03 What you are entitled to see

Ask the detective or advocate

  • Whether the police department has its own victim advocate. Many do, separate from the prosecutor's.
  • When the medical examiner's findings and a final death certificate will be available.
  • Which of your loved one's belongings are being held as evidence, and whether anything that is not evidence can be returned now.
  • Whether your state's victims' rights apply yet, or only once someone is charged.

Ask for the answers by email, so you have them in writing.

Records and rights

Police reports are public records in most states, but most states let police withhold records that are part of an active criminal investigation. In practice, the full file usually stays closed until the case is charged, resolved, or no longer active. You may still be able to get a basic incident report now. Ask.

Victims' rights start at different points in different states. In some they begin when the crime happens. In many, the strongest rights, such as notice of court hearings, start once someone is arrested or charged.

Crime victim compensation does not wait for an arrest. State programs generally require only that the crime was reported and that you cooperate with police, so you can apply now.

04 Talking about the case

Public attention can help an unsolved case. It can also hurt one. The difference is usually in the details.

1

Reporters

You do not have to talk to anyone. If you want to, a few words about who your loved one was are safe. Check with the detective before you say anything about how they died.

2

Social media

Do not post details of the scene, the injuries or the evidence, even ones you were told privately. Do not name a suspect. A photograph and a request for anyone with information to call the detective is generally safe.

3

Tips and rewards

Send every tip you hear to the detective, in writing. Ask whether there is a reward, and whether a local Crime Stoppers program can take anonymous tips and offer one. In Oregon, the statewide program closed in 2026, so see the Oregon guide.

Leave the investigating to detectives

Families often hear rumors before police do. Pass them on with names and dates, and let the detective decide what to do with them. Confronting someone yourself can put you in danger, and it can damage the case.

05 Keeping track

The log to keep

  • Every call, email and visit: the date, who it was with, and what was said.
  • The case number, and the names and direct lines of the detective and every advocate.
  • Every tip you passed on, and when.
  • The names of any reporters you spoke with.

Keep a copy with a second family member. If the case goes cold, this log may be the most complete record of the family's side of it.

If months pass

If the investigation stalls, ask in writing whether the case has moved to a cold case unit, who is assigned to it now, and when it will next be reviewed. Ask what kind of information would help.

Families are often the reason old cases get reopened. The cold case guide covers what to do once it has been years.

You do not have to wait this out alone. Parents of Murdered Children runs peer support groups, and many members are living with an unsolved case.

06 Common questions

Questions about open homicide investigations

Why won't the police tell us anything about the investigation?

Usually to protect the case. Detectives hold back details that only the person responsible would know, and anything shared with family can end up in public. Ask for one named contact and a regular check-in schedule, and ask what you may share. If you get no response at all, write to the detective's supervisor.

Can the family get the police report in a homicide case?

Usually not the full file while the case is open. Most states let police withhold records from an active criminal investigation. You may be able to get a basic incident report now, and more once the case is resolved or no longer active. Ask the detective or the department's records office what your state allows.

How long do homicide investigations take?

There is no standard length. Some cases are charged within days. Others take months for lab results and warrants, and some stay open for years. There is generally no statute of limitations for murder, so a case can be charged whenever the evidence is there.

Do victims' rights apply before anyone is arrested?

Some do, depending on the state. In some states rights begin when the crime happens. In many, the right to notice of hearings and to be heard begins once someone is charged. Ask a victim advocate which rights apply now where you live.

Can we apply for crime victim compensation if no one has been arrested?

Yes. State compensation programs generally do not require an arrest or a conviction. They usually require that the crime was reported to police and that you cooperate with the investigation. Filing deadlines are set state by state, so apply early.

Should we talk to the media about an unsolved murder?

It is your choice, and it can help. Keep to who your loved one was and a request for information, with the detective's number. Ask the detective first before mentioning how they died or who might be responsible.
