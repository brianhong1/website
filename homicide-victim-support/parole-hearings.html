Guide

Parole hearings, years later

If the sentence for your loved one's murder allows parole, the parole board will usually notify registered victims before a hearing and let the family be heard, in writing, in person or by video. Notices go to the address on file, so register with the state corrections department and parole board yourself, and update your address every time you move.

After sentencing, in states with parole

Sentencing can feel like the end. For many families it is the start of a second, quieter process that comes back every few years, sometimes for decades. Nobody calls to remind you. The families who are heard are the ones the board can find.

Where parole falls in the case
At a glance
Who decides A state parole board, not the judge or the prosecutor.
When Once the minimum term is served, often decades after sentencing. If parole is denied, again every few years.
How In writing, in person, or by video or phone, depending on the board.
Do now Register for notification, and keep your address current.

01

How a sentence reaches parole

Whether there will ever be a hearing depends on the sentence and the state.

  • 01 Sentences with a minimum term Many murder sentences are a range, such as 15 years to life. Once the minimum is served, the parole board decides whether the person is released. If parole is denied, the board sets the next review, often a few years out.
  • 02 Sentences with no parole Life without parole means there will be no parole hearing, and so does a death sentence. Clemency and commutation are separate, rare processes with their own notice rules.
  • 03 Places that ended parole The federal system ended parole for crimes committed on or after November 1, 1987, and a number of states have ended it for most crimes. Even there, people sentenced under older law, and some murder and life sentences, can still come before a board.
  • 04 Ask for the date now At sentencing, ask the prosecutor or the advocate when the person will first be eligible for parole, and write it down where the rest of the family can find it. That is the date to plan around.
02 Make sure the notice reaches you

You almost always have to sign up for notification yourself. Being on the list at sentencing does not mean you will still be on it in twenty years.

1

Sign up for custody alerts

Most states use VINE, a free and confidential service at vinelink.com, for alerts about transfers, release and escape. In some states VINE does not cover parole hearings, so register with the parole board too.

2

Register with the parole board

Ask the prosecutor's advocate or the corrections department's victim services office how to register for parole notices in your state. Some boards need a written request on their own form.

3

Keep your address current

Notices go to the last address on file. Every time you move, update the corrections department, the parole board and the prosecutor's office. Every time.

Name a second family member as a contact too, so a notice is not lost if one of you moves or can no longer carry it.

03 Being heard

Ask the board's victim services office

  • How and when to send a written statement, and whether it can be kept from the incarcerated person.
  • Whether you can appear in person, by video or by phone, and how long you may speak.
  • Whether the incarcerated person will be in the room when you speak.
  • Whether other relatives and friends can send letters or speak too.

Boards set their own deadlines, often weeks before the hearing. Ask as soon as you get the notice.

Your statement

Start from your sentencing statement, if you gave one. Then add what has happened since: the years, the milestones missed, the effects that have not faded. The board already has the facts of the crime. What it does not have is what the years have been like for your family.

Boards generally weigh the crime, the person's record in prison, their plans for release, and what victims and the prosecutor tell them. Your statement is one part of that, and it counts.

Many boards let a victim ask that their statement or contact details be kept confidential. Ask in writing, and say why.

04 Support around the hearing

You do not have to face it alone, and you do not have to do all of it yourself.

1

A victim advocate

The prosecutor's office or the corrections department often has an advocate who will explain the hearing and sometimes go with you.

2

The prosecutor

In many states the prosecutor's office can send the board its own letter, or appear. Ask whether they will, and ask early.

3

Other families

Parents of Murdered Children runs a Parole Block Program. At a family's request, it circulates petitions asking that the person serve the sentence the court imposed.

05 After the board decides

The board can grant parole, deny it and set the next review date, or postpone. Ask how the decision will reach you, and how soon.

If parole is denied, the cycle starts again. Write down the next review date and keep it with the case file, so it does not depend on one person's memory.

If parole is granted, ask about the release conditions, such as no contact with your family or limits on where the person may live, and who to call if a condition is broken.

Rights at parole are set by each state. The Ohio guide, the Oregon guide, and which rights apply to your case cover the details, including the federal Crime Victims' Rights Act, which covers any parole proceeding in a federal case.

06 Common questions

Questions about parole hearings

Are victims' families notified of parole hearings?

Usually, if you register. Most states notify victims who have asked for notice, at the last address on file. Register with the state corrections department and the parole board, sign up for VINE custody alerts where your state uses them, and update your address every time you move.

Can the family speak at a parole hearing?

In most states, yes. Families can usually send a written statement and may be able to speak in person, by video or by phone. Some boards hear victims separately from the incarcerated person. Ask the parole board's victim services office what your state allows.

How often does someone convicted of murder come up for parole?

It depends on the sentence and the state. The first hearing comes once the minimum term is served. If parole is denied, the board sets the next review, often a few years later. A person serving life without parole does not get parole hearings.

Can I keep my parole statement confidential?

Often, yes. Many parole boards let victims ask that their statement or contact information be kept from the incarcerated person. Ask in writing and say why. Rules differ by state, so confirm with the board before you send it.

Can I reuse my victim impact statement at a parole hearing?

Yes, and it is a good place to start. Add what has changed since sentencing. The board already has the facts of the crime. What it does not have is what the years since have meant for your family.

What is the POMC Parole Block Program?

A program run by Parents of Murdered Children. At a family's request, it writes and circulates petitions asking a parole board to keep the person in prison for the sentence the court imposed. It does not lobby for longer sentences.
