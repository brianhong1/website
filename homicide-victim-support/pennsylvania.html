State guide

Your rights in Pennsylvania

Families of murder victims in Pennsylvania have rights under the Crime Victims Act, not the state constitution: notice of key proceedings, not being excluded from court, giving prior comment on plea changes, a written and oral victim impact statement at sentencing, restitution, and input on parole and pardons. Since 2022, a victim has legal standing to enforce these rights in court.

Crime Victims Act, Act 111 of 1998, amended by Act 77 of 2022

Pennsylvania voters approved Marsy's Law in 2019, but the state Supreme Court struck it down before it took effect. Your rights here come from statute, and a 2022 law gave you standing to enforce them. Here is what that means for a homicide victim's family, in plain words.

01

What you are entitled to

The Act counts a homicide victim's family members as victims, and it names stepbrothers and stepsisters, stepchildren, stepparents and a fiancé or fiancée as family. One family member is identified to receive communications (Section 103). Decide who that will be and tell the prosecutor in writing.

  • 01 To receive basic information about services available to crime victims Section 201(1).
  • 02 To be notified of significant actions and proceedings in the case, including whether bail was granted Section 201(2). Keep the prosecutor and the clerk of courts updated with your contact information so notices reach you.
  • 03 Not to be excluded from any criminal proceeding unless the court finds your testimony would be materially altered by hearing other testimony Section 201(2.1). The court must try to allow the fullest attendance possible, consider alternatives, and state any reason for exclusion on the record. You can also comment on the defendant's bail conditions.
  • 04 To be accompanied at all criminal proceedings by a family member, a victim advocate or another support person Section 201(3).
  • 05 To submit prior comment to the prosecutor on any reduction or dropping of a charge, or a change of plea Section 201(4). This is your input on a plea deal. It is a right to be consulted beforehand, not to decide.
  • 06 To offer prior comment on the sentence, including a written and oral victim impact statement Section 201(5). The written statement goes into the presentence report, and the court must consider it in sentencing. See how to write one.
  • 07 To be restored, as far as possible, to your financial position before the crime through restitution, compensation and the prompt return of property Section 201(6). Under 18 Pa.C.S. 1106(c), the court must order full restitution regardless of the defendant's current financial resources. Keep every receipt from the first week on.
  • 08 When the offender goes to state prison, to comment on and be told of release decisions, including parole and pardon Section 201(7). You are also entitled to immediate notice of an escape and recapture.
  • 09 Not to have the prosecution claim you waived a right unless you knowingly agreed Section 233, added in 2022.
02 When a right is ignored

In 2022, Act 77 added Section 231 to the Crime Victims Act. A victim, or the Commonwealth at a victim's request, now has legal standing to assert and enforce any right in the Act, or any other right the law gives victims, in a trial or appellate court or before an official body with jurisdiction over the case. Section 232 says this does not make you a party, gives the defendant no grounds for relief, and creates no claim for damages.

1

Put it in writing, the same day

Email the district attorney's victim witness office. Name the right and the section of the Crime Victims Act, the date it was missed, and what you want done. A dated email is the record that matters months later.

2

Use Section 231

Ask the prosecutor to raise the violation with the court, as Section 231 allows the Commonwealth to do at your request. If it will not, you or your own attorney can raise it, in the trial court, the appellate court, or before the parole board if that is where the problem is.

3

If you want legal help

You do not need a lawyer to use these rights. Section 231 gives you standing to raise them yourself, or you can ask the district attorney to. If you want someone on your side, keep in mind the district attorney represents the Commonwealth, not your family, and a private attorney can assert your rights directly. For parole and pardons, the Office of Victim Advocate represents victims' interests at no cost. It is listed below.

Why there is no Marsy's Law here

Pennsylvania voters approved a Marsy's Law amendment in November 2019, but a court blocked the results from being certified. In League of Women Voters of Pennsylvania v. Degraffenreid, decided December 21, 2021, the Pennsylvania Supreme Court held that the amendment bundled several unrelated changes into one ballot question, which violates the constitution's separate-vote requirement. The rights on this page are statutory, and the legislature can change them by ordinary law.

03 Compensation in Pennsylvania

The program

The Victims Compensation Assistance Program is run by the Pennsylvania Commission on Crime and Delinquency under Chapter 7 of the Crime Victims Act. For a homicide it can help the person who paid for the funeral, surviving family with counseling, dependents with loss of support, and the person who paid for crime scene cleanup. In a homicide, the victim's own conduct is not held against family members claiming counseling and funeral costs.

File within five years of the crime, of the death, or of the discovery and identification of the body of a murder victim (Section 702(b)). Total awards are capped at $35,000, with some costs like counseling counted separately (Section 707).

PCCD pays up to $6,500 per victim for funeral and burial expenses, according to its funeral expenses guide. Payments of $5,000 or less owed directly to you are released within two business days of approval.

Before you file

Gather the itemized funeral contract in the name of whoever paid, canceled checks or funeral home receipts, and any life insurance or Social Security death benefits received. PCCD asks for all three before it pays a funeral claim. Your district attorney's victim witness office can help you file.

File even if something is missing, and ask what can be supplemented afterward. A filed claim with gaps beats a complete claim filed past the deadline.

The first two weeks, including what to keep

A separate deadline: wrongful death. A civil lawsuit runs on its own clock, apart from compensation and the criminal case. In Pennsylvania that is two years from the death, and a claim against a government unit needs written notice within six months. Wrongful death deadlines by state

04 If the case is unsolved

Pennsylvania has no statute of limitations on murder. Under 42 Pa.C.S. 5551, a prosecution for murder or voluntary manslaughter may be commenced at any time, so the case does not expire.

1

Keep one named contact

Get the detective's name, direct number, email and the case number in writing. When that detective retires or moves units, ask who owns the case now and write it down. Send every tip you hear to that person, in writing, so it lands in the file.

2

Compensation can wait for a body

If your loved one was missing, the five-year compensation clock can run from the discovery and identification of the body, not from the disappearance (Section 702(b)). Keep receipts from the search itself, and ask PCCD what can be claimed.

3

Before you fund a reward or hire an investigator

Talk to a Pennsylvania attorney and the detective first. A reward offer can be a binding promise, so the wording matters: who qualifies, whether it pays on arrest or conviction, and what happens if no one claims it. A private investigator should be licensed in Pennsylvania and work with the detective, because interviewing witnesses first can hand the defense something to attack.

Parole and pardons

In Pennsylvania, first degree murder carries life imprisonment or death and second degree murder carries life imprisonment (18 Pa.C.S. 1102), and the Parole Board cannot parole anyone serving life imprisonment (61 Pa.C.S. 6137(a)(1)). For those cases, the path to release is a commutation through the Board of Pardons and the Governor. Second degree (felony) murder is changing. In Commonwealth v. Lee, decided March 26, 2026, the Pennsylvania Supreme Court held that a mandatory life without parole sentence for second degree murder violates Article I, Section 13 of the state constitution. Judges must now weigh each person's role and intent, and can still impose life in a fitting case. The court gave the General Assembly until July 24, 2026 to write a new law and refused to extend that deadline. As of October 2026 no replacement law had passed, and the court had not decided whether the ruling reaches the roughly 1,100 people already serving that sentence. If your family's case was second degree murder, even one from years ago, ask the district attorney's victim witness office and the Office of Victim Advocate to notify you of any resentencing. A resentencing is a sentencing, and you can give an impact statement there. Third degree murder carries a term of years, so parole is possible after the minimum. Either way, register with the Office of Victim Advocate, which represents victims before the Parole Board and can petition that an offender not be paroled. How to prepare for a parole hearing

06 Common questions

Questions about Pennsylvania victims' rights

What rights do murder victims' families have in Pennsylvania?

Under the Crime Victims Act, 18 P.S. 11.101 and following, a murder victim's family members can be notified of significant proceedings, are not excluded from court except in narrow cases, can give prior comment on plea changes and sentencing, can give a written and oral victim impact statement, are entitled to restitution, and can comment on parole and pardon decisions.

Does Pennsylvania have Marsy's Law?

No. Voters approved a Marsy's Law amendment in 2019, but in League of Women Voters of Pennsylvania v. Degraffenreid, decided December 21, 2021, the Pennsylvania Supreme Court held it violated the separate-vote requirement, and it never took effect. Victims' rights in Pennsylvania come from the Crime Victims Act.

Can a victim's family enforce their rights in Pennsylvania?

Yes. Section 231 of the Crime Victims Act, added in 2022, gives a victim, or the Commonwealth at the victim's request, legal standing to assert and enforce victims' rights in a trial or appellate court or before an official body with jurisdiction over the case. It does not make the victim a party.

Who counts as family of a homicide victim in Pennsylvania?

The Crime Victims Act defines a victim to include a family member of a homicide victim, and it expressly includes stepbrothers, stepsisters, stepchildren, stepparents and a fiancé or fiancée. One family member is identified to receive communications.

Does Pennsylvania pay for a murder victim's funeral?

The Pennsylvania Commission on Crime and Delinquency pays up to $6,500 per victim for funeral and burial expenses, within a $35,000 overall cap. Claims are due within five years of the crime, the death, or the discovery and identification of the body. Call 800-233-2339.

Can someone convicted of murder get parole in Pennsylvania?

Not if they are serving life. 61 Pa.C.S. 6137 bars parole for anyone serving life, so release requires a commutation through the Board of Pardons and the Governor. First degree murder carries life or death. Second degree (felony) murder is changing: in Commonwealth v. Lee, decided March 26, 2026, the Pennsylvania Supreme Court held that a mandatory life without parole sentence for second degree murder violates the state constitution, and judges must now weigh each person's role. As of October 2026 the General Assembly had not passed a replacement sentencing law, and the court had not decided whether the ruling reaches the roughly 1,100 people already serving that sentence. If your family's case was second degree murder, ask the district attorney's victim witness office to notify you of any resentencing, where you can give an impact statement. Third degree murder carries a term of years, so parole is possible after the minimum.

Is there a statute of limitations on murder in Pennsylvania?

No. Under 42 Pa.C.S. 5551, a prosecution for murder or voluntary manslaughter may be commenced at any time. An unsolved homicide in Pennsylvania does not expire.
