Guide

When the killer is family

A person who feloniously and intentionally kills someone cannot inherit from them, collect their life insurance, or take over property the two of them owned together. The law treats the killer as having died first. This is called the slayer rule. Nearly every state has it in a statute, and the rest apply it through court decisions. It is rarely automatic. Someone usually has to raise it with the probate court or the insurance company.

For families where the person responsible is a spouse, child, parent or other relative

This is the hardest version of an already unbearable thing. You have lost someone, and the person accused of it is someone you know, may love, and may be named in the will, on the insurance policy and on the deed to the house. This page covers the money and the paperwork, so that part does not go wrong while you are dealing with everything else.

The full guide for families after a homicide

01

What the slayer rule is

One sentence of law, and a long list of things it touches.

  • 01 The killer is treated as having died first That is how most statutes put it. Whatever the person would have received because of the death goes instead to whoever was next in line: the other heirs, the backup beneficiary on the policy, or the estate.
  • 02 It reaches almost everything that passes at death A share under a will. A share under the inheritance laws when there is no will. A trust. Life insurance. A pay-on-death bank account. A retirement account. The survivor's right to a jointly owned house. Most statutes also cancel the will's choice of the killer as executor or trustee.
  • 03 The idea is older than the statutes In 1886 the U.S. Supreme Court refused to let a life insurance policy pay the man who murdered the insured (Mutual Life Insurance Co. v. Armstrong). In 1889 New York's highest court refused to let a grandson inherit under the will of the grandfather he poisoned (Riggs v. Palmer, 115 N.Y. 506). Both courts said the same thing: no one may profit from their own crime.
  • 04 Nearly every state has now written it down Many states follow the model in Section 2-803 of the Uniform Probate Code. New York still relies mainly on court decisions. New Hampshire, which had done the same, passed a statute in 2025 (S.B. 148). The details differ more than you would expect, and the table below shows how.
  • 05 It does not run by itself No office checks whether an heir or beneficiary caused the death. A bank or insurer that pays the named person before anyone objects is usually protected. The rule works when someone in the family, or the estate's representative, raises it in writing and, if needed, in court.

03

When there is no conviction, or not for murder

The criminal case and the inheritance question are decided separately, on different standards.

  • 01 A conviction usually settles it In most states a final conviction for an intentional killing is conclusive. The probate court does not hear the evidence again. Some states wait until the appeals are finished before the conviction counts.
  • 02 Without a conviction, a civil court can usually decide Most states let the probate court, or another civil court, decide whether the person committed the killing. The standard is a preponderance of the evidence, which means more likely than not. That is far lower than proof beyond a reasonable doubt. Georgia asks for clear and convincing evidence. A few statutes, such as Kentucky's and Ohio's, are tied to what happens in the criminal case.
  • 03 An acquittal does not always end the question Because the civil standard is lower, a person found not guilty at trial can still be found responsible in probate court in the states that allow a civil finding. The same is true when charges are dropped or never filed.
  • 04 A plea to a lesser charge may or may not count Most statutes cover intentional killings only. A reckless or negligent death usually falls outside them. But states draw the line in different places. Georgia, Indiana and Virginia name voluntary manslaughter. Arizona's and Colorado's statutes name manslaughter. Ohio's reaches most involuntary manslaughter. If a plea offer is on the table, ask the prosecutor's victim advocate what charge it is for, and ask a probate attorney whether that charge still triggers the rule.
  • 05 Insanity findings are handled differently from state to state Arizona, Ohio, Oregon and Washington apply the rule to a person found not guilty by reason of insanity or guilty except insane. Indiana counts a verdict of guilty but mentally ill. North Carolina's statute says a person found not guilty by reason of insanity is not a slayer, and Mississippi's Supreme Court has held that a person who was legally insane did not act wilfully.
  • 06 When the killer was a juvenile Ohio and North Carolina say outright that a juvenile found delinquent for the killing is covered. Elsewhere it depends on the wording of the statute and on what a civil court finds.
  • 07 When the killer has died After a murder-suicide, or when the accused dies before trial, there will be no conviction. In the states that allow a civil finding, the court can still decide the question so that the property goes to the right people. Virginia's statute says so in terms.
04 The estate and probate court

What to do about the estate

Probate is the court process that settles what a person owned and owed. In these cases, the person with the strongest claim on paper may be the person who caused the death. The court can deal with that, but it has to be asked.

1

Who is in charge of the estate

A surviving spouse or a person named in the will normally has first claim to run the estate. Many slayer statutes cancel that. California, South Carolina and Tennessee, for example, revoke a will's nomination of the killer as executor or personal representative. If the person has not been convicted, you can object to their appointment and ask the court to appoint someone else, or a neutral administrator, while the criminal case is pending.

2

Ask the court to hold things in place

A criminal case can take years, and money can be spent in that time, including on a defense. Probate courts can order assets frozen or held until the question is decided. Some states build this in. Pennsylvania requires the estate's representative to hold in escrow whatever a person charged with the homicide would receive. New York lets the criminal court freeze a joint bank account during a murder trial.

3

Put banks and insurers on written notice

Several statutes, including those in Alabama, South Carolina and Tennessee, protect a bank or insurer that pays the named person before it receives written notice of a claim. A short letter saying the death is a homicide, that the named person is a suspect or defendant, and that the estate objects to payment, changes that. Send it by certified mail and keep a copy.

4

The house and joint accounts

When two people own property with a right of survivorship, the survivor normally takes all of it. The slayer rule stops that. In most states the victim's share goes to the victim's estate and the killer keeps only their own share. Some states go further. Under Virginia's statute, property a married couple held together passes to the victim's estate as though the killer had died first.

5

Who takes instead

Usually the person next in line: the backup beneficiary, the other heirs, or the people named in the will. Because the killer is treated as having died first, the killer's own children can end up taking that share in many states. North Carolina and Louisiana say so in their statutes. This matters most when those children are also the victim's children or grandchildren.

6

If the killer died too

The killer's estate is then a separate estate with its own heirs. Your family may have a wrongful death claim against it, and every state sets a short deadline for filing claims against an estate, often only a few months. South Carolina's statute treats the victim as the survivor if the killer dies within 120 hours. Wrongful death deadlines by state

05 Life insurance and other benefits

Life insurance, retirement plans and survivor benefits

Much of what a person leaves behind never passes through a will. It goes straight to whoever is named on a form. The slayer rule reaches those payments too, and federal benefits have their own versions of it.

1

Why the claim is on hold

When a death certificate says homicide, a life insurer will usually not pay until it knows the beneficiary was not involved. That can delay payment to a beneficiary who had nothing to do with the death. Ask the insurer, in writing, exactly what it is waiting for. Often it is a letter from the detective or the prosecutor saying you are not a suspect. Ask the victim advocate to help you get it.

2

When the insurer goes to court

If the named beneficiary is a suspect or a defendant, the insurer will often pay the money into court and ask a judge to decide who gets it. This is called interpleader. You will be served with papers, and you need to answer them to keep your claim. It is a good moment to hire a probate or insurance attorney.

3

Who gets the money instead

The backup beneficiary named on the policy, if there is one. If there is none, the money usually goes to the insured person's estate. Texas sends it to the insured's nearest relative. Check for policies you may not know about: through an employer, a union, a credit union, a mortgage or a credit card.

4

Life insurance and retirement plans through work

Most workplace plans are governed by a federal law called ERISA, and courts have not finally settled whether it overrides state slayer statutes. In practice the killer loses either way. The Supreme Court noted in 2001 that the principle behind these statutes is well established (Egelhoff v. Egelhoff). The Seventh Circuit held in 2018 that ERISA does not override the Illinois statute (Laborers' Pension Fund v. Miscevic). The Sixth Circuit held in 2024 that federal common law bars a killer from the benefits whether or not the state statute applies (Standard Insurance Co. v. Guy).

5

Social Security survivor benefits

A person convicted of a felony of intentionally causing someone's death cannot receive survivor benefits on that person's earnings record. The same applies to a juvenile found to have intentionally caused the death (20 CFR 404.305(b)). Children and other survivors who were not involved keep their own eligibility.

6

Military and veterans' benefits

Servicemembers' and Veterans' Group Life Insurance is not paid to a person convicted of intentionally and wrongfully killing the insured, or found in a civil case to have done so (38 CFR 9.5(e)). A person who intentionally and wrongfully caused a veteran's death cannot receive VA pension, compensation or dependency and indemnity compensation because of it (38 CFR 3.11).

The civil case is separate. The slayer rule only keeps the killer from receiving money. A wrongful death lawsuit is how a family seeks money from the person responsible, and it has its own deadline. Wrongful death claims after a homicide

06 Things that come up in the first days

Some of this cannot wait for a court to sort out who inherits.

1

Who decides about the funeral

The law normally gives a spouse, then other next of kin, the right to decide about burial or cremation. Some states take that right away from a person accused of the killing. In Ohio it is lost on a charge of murder, aggravated murder or voluntary manslaughter (R.C. 2108.77). In Washington it is lost on an arrest or charge for murder or first degree manslaughter (RCW 68.50.160). In Texas it is lost on an arrest for a homicide involving family violence (Health & Safety Code 711.002(l)). Tell the funeral home about the arrest or charge. If there is a dispute, a probate court can decide quickly.

2

The children

When one parent has killed the other, someone has to care for the children right away. A grandparent, aunt, uncle or other relative can ask the family or probate court for emergency custody or guardianship. A parent who is charged does not lose parental rights automatically, and a court decides custody and contact. Money that goes to a child, from an inheritance, an insurance policy or Social Security, has to be managed by an adult. Ask the court and each agency who that will be, and object if it is the accused parent.

3

Crime victim compensation still applies

Being related to the person who did this, or having lived with them, does not disqualify you. Federal law says a state program may not deny compensation for those reasons, except under rules meant to keep the money from benefiting the offender (34 U.S.C. 20102(b)(7)). Apply for the funeral, counseling and lost support, and tell the program how the payment will be kept away from the person responsible. How compensation works

You are still the victim's family, and the rights that come with that are still yours, even when you are also related to the defendant. Relatives often end up on different sides. 07 Who to call

A probate attorney

Look for one in the county where your relative lived, and ask whether they have handled a slayer case or a contested estate. Your state bar runs a lawyer referral service, and many offer a low-cost first meeting. If you cannot afford an attorney, call legal aid and the probate court's self-help office and ask what they can do.

The prosecutor's victim advocate

Ask them to tell you when charges are filed, what a plea offer would be for, and when the conviction is final. Ask for a certified copy of the judgment when there is one. The probate court and the insurer will both want it.

Help in your state, and at any hour

If an insurer will not explain a delay, your state insurance department takes complaints: find your department. The Office for Victims of Crime lists each state's compensation program: ovc.ojp.gov. 08 Common questions

Questions about the slayer rule

Can a murderer inherit from their victim?

No. Under the slayer rule, a person who feloniously and intentionally kills someone cannot inherit from them, by will or without one. The law treats the killer as having died before the victim, so the property goes to the next people in line. Nearly every state has this rule in a statute.

Can a killer collect the victim's life insurance?

No. A beneficiary who intentionally killed the insured person cannot collect. The money goes to the backup beneficiary, or to the insured's estate if there is none. Insurers usually hold a claim while a beneficiary is under suspicion, and often pay the money into court so a judge can decide.

Does the slayer rule require a criminal conviction?

Usually not. A conviction settles the question, but most states also let a civil court decide by a preponderance of the evidence, which means more likely than not. A person who was acquitted or never charged can still be barred that way. A few states, such as Kentucky, tie the rule to a conviction.

Does the slayer rule apply to manslaughter or an insanity verdict?

It depends on the state. Most statutes cover intentional killings only, so a reckless or negligent death is usually outside them. Some states name voluntary manslaughter, and Arizona and Colorado name manslaughter. Arizona, Ohio, Oregon and Washington apply the rule after an insanity verdict. North Carolina and Mississippi do not.

Who gets the property if the killer cannot?

Whoever would have taken it if the killer had died first. That is usually the backup beneficiary, the other heirs, or the others named in the will. In many states the killer's own children can take that share, which matters when they are also the victim's children.

What happens to a house the killer and victim owned together?

The killer does not take the whole house as the survivor. In most states the joint ownership is split, the victim's share goes to the victim's estate, and the killer keeps only their own share. A few states go further and limit even that.

What if it was a murder-suicide?

The rule still applies. There will be no conviction, but in most states a civil court can find that the person committed the killing, so the victim's property does not pass through the killer's estate. Claims against the killer's estate have short deadlines, often only a few months.

Do we have to do anything, or is it automatic?

It is rarely automatic. Tell the probate court, the insurer and the banks in writing that the death is a homicide and that you object to payment to the person responsible. An insurer or bank that pays before it gets notice is usually protected. A probate attorney can file the papers.
