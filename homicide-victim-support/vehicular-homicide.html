Guide

When the crash was a crime

When a drunk, drugged or reckless driver kills someone, the death is a homicide, whatever name the charge carries: vehicular manslaughter, DUI manslaughter, intoxication manslaughter, homicide by vehicle. Your family are the victims of a crime. That brings victims' rights in the criminal case, crime victim compensation, and the chance to speak at sentencing.

For families of someone killed by a drunk, drugged or reckless driver

You may not think of yourself as a homicide victim's family. Most families in your place do not. The officer said crash, the insurance company said accident, and nobody mentioned that the laws written for a murder victim's family may also be written for yours.

01

People will call it an accident

It is the word everyone reaches for. It is not the word the law uses.

  • 01 Homicide means one person caused another's death It does not mean murder, and it does not require that the driver meant to hurt anyone. Choosing to drive drunk, drugged or recklessly is the crime. The death is what the law holds the driver responsible for.
  • 02 The charge names differ, the idea does not Gross vehicular manslaughter in California. Intoxication manslaughter in Texas. DUI manslaughter in Florida. Homicide by vehicle in Georgia and Pennsylvania. Each one is a homicide charge written for a death on the road.
  • 03 Your family is one of thousands In 2024, 11,904 people were killed in U.S. crashes involving an alcohol-impaired driver. That is 30 percent of all traffic deaths that year, according to the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration. The count does not include deaths caused by drivers who were drugged or reckless but under the alcohol limit.
  • 04 When an office says a program is for crime victims, that means you Some families cannot stand the word accident. Others find the word homicide too heavy to say. You do not have to choose. What matters is that you do not rule yourself out of help because of a word.

Not in one of these states? Your state has its own name for these charges. Ask the prosecutor's victim advocate for the exact charge and statute number, and write both down next to the case number.

03 The same rights apply

Victims' rights laws are written for victims of crime. They do not say murder. If the driver is charged with a felony, your family is usually covered the same way any homicide victim's family is. Many of these rights only work if you ask for them, and it is best to ask in writing.

1

To be told

Ask for notice of every hearing, of any release from jail, and of any plea offer. Tell the prosecutor's victim advocate that you are the victim's family and that you want to be notified. Which rights apply to your case

2

To be heard before a plea

Most criminal cases end in a plea agreement, not a trial. In many states you have a right to talk with the prosecutor before an offer is made. Tell the advocate, in writing, that you want that conversation.

3

To speak at sentencing

You can tell the judge what this death has done to your family, in writing, in person, or both. Where the law gives the judge a wide range, your statement is part of what the judge weighs. Writing an impact statement

Restitution is part of this too. A judge can order the driver to repay documented losses, such as the funeral, as part of the sentence. Keep every receipt. Your state guide lists the rights by number and the office that enforces them. Find your state guide

04

How these cases run differently

The rights are the same. The shape of the case is not.

  • 01 The arrest may come weeks or months later Prosecutors often wait for the blood test to come back from the lab and for the crash reconstruction to be finished before they file charges. A driver who went home that night has not been cleared. Ask the detective or the advocate when the results are expected and who will review them.
  • 02 The driver may be out while the case is pending Many people charged with these crimes are released on bail before trial. You can ask the prosecutor to request conditions, such as no driving, alcohol monitoring and no contact with your family.
  • 03 Expect a plea offer Few of these cases go to trial. A plea is not always a bad result, because it is certain and it spares your family a trial. But you should hear about it before it is final, not after.
  • 04 The sentence may be shorter than you expect The ranges in the table above are far below the sentences for murder, and time off for good behavior can shorten them further. Register for release notification now, through VINE or your state's own system, so that a release or a parole hearing does not catch you by surprise. What to expect at a parole hearing
  • 05 If the driver died too There is no one to prosecute, so there is no criminal case and no court advocate assigned to you. You may still qualify for crime victim compensation, and a civil claim against the driver's estate and insurance still exists. Call your state's compensation program directly.
  • 06 If the driver fled or was never found A hit and run death is an open investigation. What to expect while a case is open. Check your own auto policy as well. Uninsured motorist coverage may apply even when the driver has not been identified.
05 Money, and the clocks on it

There are more sources of money after a crash than after most homicides, and more ways to lose one by waiting or by signing too early.

1

Crime victim compensation

Federal law requires every state compensation program that takes federal funding to cover victims of drunk driving (34 U.S.C. 20102(b)(1)). These programs commonly help with funeral costs, counseling and lost income. If the driver was reckless but sober, coverage depends on your state, so apply and ask. How compensation works

2

Insurance, theirs and yours

The driver's auto liability policy is usually the first source of money, and its limit may be low. Your own uninsured or underinsured motorist coverage, or the policy of the person who died, may add to it. Talk to a civil attorney once before you give a recorded statement or sign a release. A signed release usually ends the claim for good.

3

Others who may share responsibility

In many states a bar, restaurant or store that served someone who was clearly drunk can be sued. In some, so can a host who gave alcohol to a minor. These are called dram shop and social host laws. They differ widely, and some states set short notice deadlines. If the driver was working at the time, the employer may be responsible too.

4

Bankruptcy does not erase it

Under federal law, a debt for a death caused by driving while intoxicated is not wiped out when the driver files for bankruptcy (11 U.S.C. 523(a)(9)). A judgment can still be hard to collect, but it does not go away.

The civil deadline is separate. A wrongful death claim usually must be filed within two years of the death, and in most states that clock keeps running during the criminal case. Wrongful death deadlines by state

Tell the compensation program about any insurance payment, and tell your attorney about any compensation award. One can reduce the other, and an undisclosed payment is a common reason a claim is clawed back later.

06 Who to call

MADD Victim Help Line

1-877-MADD-HELP (1-877-623-3435), 24 hours a day. Mothers Against Drunk Driving has victim advocates who offer emotional support, go to court with you and help you prepare an impact statement, at no cost. madd.org/victim-assistance

The prosecutor's victim advocate

Nearly every prosecutor's or district attorney's office has one. Ask for them by that title. They can tell you the charge, the next court date, and how to put your requests for notice in writing.

Help in your state

The federal Office for Victims of Crime lists each state's compensation and victim assistance programs. ovc.ojp.gov. For a referral in any state, call VictimConnect at 1-855-484-2846. If the grief becomes a crisis, call or text 988 at any hour.

07 Common questions

Questions about vehicular homicide and DUI deaths

Is a drunk driving death a homicide?

Yes. Homicide means one person caused the death of another. It does not require intent to kill. States charge these deaths under names such as vehicular homicide, vehicular manslaughter, DUI manslaughter or intoxication manslaughter, and the family is the family of a crime victim.

Do we have victims' rights if the charge is vehicular manslaughter?

Usually, yes. Victims' rights laws cover victims of crime and do not depend on the word murder. If the driver is charged with a felony, the family generally has the same rights to be notified, to be heard and to speak at sentencing. Many of those rights must be requested, so ask the prosecutor's victim advocate in writing.

Does crime victim compensation cover a DUI death?

Yes. Federal law requires state compensation programs that receive federal funding to cover victims of drunk driving, 34 U.S.C. 20102(b)(1). Programs commonly help with funeral costs, counseling and lost income. Each state sets its own limits and filing deadline, so apply early.

Why has the driver not been arrested yet?

Prosecutors often wait for blood test results and a crash reconstruction before filing charges, and both can take weeks or months. A driver who went home after the crash has not been cleared. Ask the detective or victim advocate when results are expected and who is reviewing the case.

Can we sue if the driver was drunk?

Yes. A wrongful death lawsuit is a civil case, separate from the prosecution. It can reach the driver, the driver's insurance and sometimes a bar or host who served them. It has its own deadline, usually two years from the death, and in most states that clock keeps running during the criminal case.

What if the drunk driver died in the crash too?

There will be no criminal case, because there is no one to prosecute. The family may still qualify for crime victim compensation and can still bring a civil claim against the driver's estate and insurance. Call your state's compensation program, and ask a civil attorney about the deadline.
