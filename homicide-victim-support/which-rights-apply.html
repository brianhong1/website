Which rights apply to your case

Nearly every homicide is prosecuted by a state, so your state's victims' rights law governs your case. The federal Crime Victims' Rights Act, 18 U.S.C. § 3771, applies only when a U.S. Attorney prosecutes in federal court. In Ohio the rights come from Article I, Section 10a; in Oregon, from Article I, Sections 42 and 43.

Ohio and Oregon are here because they are written up in depth on this site. If your state is not one of them, read the federal column only if your case is federal, then ask your prosecutor's office for your state's written list of rights.

Which column is yours
State court
A county prosecutor or district attorney is handling it. Your state's law governs. This is almost every case.
Federal court
A U.S. Attorney is handling it, in a U.S. District Court. Happens with crimes on tribal land or a military installation, and some interstate and racketeering cases.
Not sure
The court name on your paperwork tells you. The victim advocate can confirm it in one call.

Does federal law apply to a murder case?

Only when the federal government brings the charges. A U.S. Attorney prosecutes in a U.S. District Court, which happens with crimes on tribal land or a military installation and in some interstate and racketeering cases. Then the Crime Victims' Rights Act, 18 U.S.C. § 3771, applies. Every other homicide is prosecuted by a county prosecutor or district attorney under state law.

Is there a national victims' rights law?

Why these columns differ

There is no national victims' rights amendment. The U.S. Constitution spells out rights for the accused and says nothing about victims, and Congress has never passed one. Every protection below exists because a state put it in its own constitution or statute, or because Congress wrote it into federal law for federal cases.

How do victims' rights compare in federal court, Ohio and Oregon?

Victims' rights compared: federal cases, Ohio and Oregon
The right Federal cases 18 U.S.C. § 3771, the Crime Victims' Rights Act Ohio Const. art. I, sec. 10a; R.C. ch. 2930 Oregon Const. art. I, secs. 42 and 43; ORS ch. 147
Protection from the accused § 3771(a)(1) The right to be reasonably protected from the accused. Sec. 10a(A)(4) Reasonable protection from the accused and from anyone acting on the accused's behalf. Sec. 43(1) Reasonable protection from the defendant throughout the case. Pretrial release decisions must rest on reasonable protection of you and the public, and murder is not bailable when the proof is evident or the presumption strong.
Notice of proceedings § 3771(a)(2) Reasonable, accurate and timely notice of any public court proceeding, any parole proceeding, and any release or escape of the accused. Sec. 10a(A)(2), (A)(5) On request, reasonable and timely notice of all public proceedings, and of any release or escape of the accused. Law enforcement and the prosecutor each carry written notice duties under R.C. 2930.04 to 2930.06. Law enforcement must notify you of your Section 42 rights as soon as reasonably practicable under ORS 147.417, including the deadline for any right that depends on a request. In a homicide, that notice goes to the next of kin.
Being present § 3771(a)(3) Not to be excluded from a public court proceeding, unless the court finds by clear and convincing evidence that your testimony would be materially altered by hearing other testimony. Sec. 10a(A)(2) Present at all public proceedings in the case. The grand jury is closed, so this does not reach it. Sec. 42(1)(a) Present at any critical stage held in open court when the defendant will be there, and told in advance on specific request. A defendant who objects to your presence at trial must file within 35 days of arraignment under ORS 147.522.
Being heard § 3771(a)(4) Reasonably heard at any public district court proceeding involving release, plea or sentencing, and at any parole proceeding. Sec. 10a(A)(3) Heard at any public proceeding involving release, plea, sentencing, disposition or parole. Sec. 42(1)(a) Heard at the pretrial release hearing and at sentencing.
Plea deals, and talking with the prosecutor § 3771(a)(5) A reasonable right to confer with the attorney for the Government. § 3771(a)(9) To be informed in a timely manner of any plea bargain or deferred prosecution agreement. Sec. 10a(A)(9) On request, to confer with the prosecutor. R.C. 2930.06 requires the prosecutor to confer with you before agreeing to a negotiated plea, amending or dismissing charges, granting pretrial diversion, or going to trial. A missed conference does not undo a plea once it is entered. Sec. 42(1)(f) Consulted, on request, about plea negotiations in any violent felony.
Restitution § 3771(a)(6) Full and timely restitution as provided in law. Sec. 10a(A)(7) Full and timely restitution from the person who committed the offense. Sec. 42(1)(d) Prompt restitution from the person convicted.
Unreasonable delay § 3771(a)(7) Proceedings free from unreasonable delay. Sec. 10a(A)(8) Proceedings free from unreasonable delay and a prompt conclusion of the case. If you object to a continuance, R.C. 2930.08 lets the court grant it only if the delay is reasonable or in the interest of justice, and the court must state its reason. ORS 147.430 Trial and sentencing conducted with all practicable speed, and a prompt final conclusion of any appeal. The only remedy is a prompt trial or sentencing. It cannot get the case dismissed.
Fairness, dignity and privacy § 3771(a)(8) To be treated with fairness and with respect for your dignity and privacy. Sec. 10a(A)(1) To be treated with fairness and respect for your safety, dignity and privacy. Not a separate listed right. Section 42 gives "due dignity and respect" as one of its purposes, and ORS 147.410 declares that victims are entitled to fair and impartial treatment.
Saying no to the defense Not enumerated in § 3771(a). Raise it through the attorney for the Government. Sec. 10a(A)(6) You may refuse a defense interview, deposition or other discovery request. A court-ordered deposition or a subpoena still applies (R.C. 2930.072). In court, you need not give your address, phone number, workplace or other locating information unless you consent or the court finds due process requires it (R.C. 2930.07). Sec. 42(1)(c) You may refuse an interview, deposition or other discovery request by the defendant or anyone acting for the defendant. This does not limit the defendant's own right to discovery from the state.
Asserting a right that was denied § 3771(d) You, your lawful representative, or the attorney for the Government may assert these rights by motion in the district court, which must decide it promptly. A denial can be taken up by writ of mandamus. The statute creates no cause of action for damages and is not a ground for a new trial. Sec. 10a(B) You, your lawful representative, or the prosecutor at your request may assert these rights in any proceeding in the case. If relief is denied, you may petition the court of appeals, which must decide promptly. R.C. 2930.011 confirms Chapter 2930 does not limit those constitutional rights. A claim process runs through ORS 147.500 to 147.550. Tell the court of a violation within 30 days of when you knew, or should have known, the facts (ORS 147.515). A motion on an issue that will affect the trial is generally due within 35 days of arraignment or the initial plea, whichever comes sooner (ORS 147.522).

A What to do when a right is ignored

What can I do if my rights as a victim are ignored?

Step one

Put it in writing, the same day

Email the prosecutor's office. Name the right, the date it was missed, and what you want done about it. A dated email is the record that matters months later.

Step two

Ask for it on the record

Every column above provides some way to raise a right in the court handling the case. A right raised in a hallway is a conversation. A right raised in open court is a ruling.

Step three

Get your own attorney

The prosecutor represents the government, not you. Nonprofit victims' rights attorneys represent families at no cost in many states. Who to call.

B Common questions

Questions about which law covers your case

How do I know if my case is in state or federal court?

Look at the court name on your paperwork. If a county prosecutor or district attorney is handling the case, it is in state court and your state's law governs. That is almost every homicide. If a U.S. Attorney is handling it in a U.S. District Court, it is federal. The victim advocate can confirm it in one call.

Can I speak at the sentencing?

Yes, in all three. Federal law, 18 U.S.C. § 3771(a)(4), gives you the right to be reasonably heard at sentencing. Ohio's Constitution, Article I, Section 10a(A)(3), does the same. In Oregon, Article I, Section 42(1)(a) gives you the right to be heard at sentencing. Advance notice of the hearing depends on your request, so ask the district attorney's office in writing.

Do I have to be told about a plea deal?

In a federal case, 18 U.S.C. § 3771(a)(9) entitles you to timely notice of any plea bargain. In Ohio, R.C. 2930.06 requires the prosecutor to confer with you before agreeing to a negotiated plea, though a missed conference does not undo a plea once entered. In Oregon, you have the right, on request, to be consulted about plea negotiations in a violent felony.

Can I refuse to talk to the defense attorney?

In Ohio and Oregon, yes. Both state constitutions let you refuse an interview, deposition or other discovery request from the defense, though in Ohio a subpoena or court-ordered deposition still applies. The federal Crime Victims' Rights Act does not list this right, so in a federal case raise it through the attorney for the Government.

Can I object if the trial keeps getting delayed?

Yes. Federal law and Ohio's Constitution both give you the right to proceedings free from unreasonable delay. In Ohio, if you object to a continuance, R.C. 2930.08 lets the court grant it only if the delay is reasonable or in the interest of justice, and the court must state its reason. Oregon's ORS 147.430 calls for trial with all practicable speed, but the remedy is a prompt trial, not dismissal.
